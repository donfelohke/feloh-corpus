## 1. Purpose and scope

This volume establishes a research framework for reconstructing the histories of peoples, lands, political formations and civilisations across the continent and its connected worlds. It asks how communities named themselves and their territories; how populations moved, interacted and changed; how institutions and states emerged; and how trade, migration, ecology, technology and knowledge connected societies.

The plural terms matter. There is no single African people, language, civilisation, political model or historical trajectory. Continental-scale synthesis is useful only when it preserves regional specificity and the evidence for local histories.

This is a framework and source map, not a completed continental history. Individual claims remain subject to source review, community interpretation and explicit evidence labels.

### Research questions

1. Which names did communities use for themselves, their neighbours, settlements, landscapes and political formations at particular times?
2. What can archaeology, historical linguistics, genetics, palaeoenvironmental research, oral history and written records each establish—and where do their limits differ?
3. How did mobility, settlement, pastoralism, agriculture, fishing, craft, urbanism and trade vary across regions and periods?
4. How did states, kingdoms, city networks, federations, chiefdoms, age-set institutions and other forms of authority develop?
5. How did African societies shape and participate in trans-Saharan, Red Sea, Nile, Mediterranean, Indian Ocean and Atlantic networks?
6. How did colonial conquest, cartography, censuses, borders and administrative classification alter political and social categories?
7. How do present-day communities understand continuity, change, ancestry, land, belonging and historical memory?

## 2. Historical method: no single archive

No one source type is sufficient for all questions. Different archives preserve different kinds of evidence and carry different biases.

| Evidence class | What it can contribute | Main cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Archaeology and material culture | Settlement, production, exchange, technology, diet, burial and landscape use | Material objects do not automatically identify a language, ethnicity or political identity |
| Historical linguistics | Language relationships, contact, borrowing and carefully bounded reconstructions | A language family is not a race, nation or unchanged people |
| Oral traditions and testimony | Community memory, political legitimacy, migration, genealogy, ecological knowledge and historical interpretation | Narratives have genres, custodians, audiences, changes and competing versions; record context and consent |
| Written records and manuscripts | Administrative, religious, commercial, diplomatic and intellectual activity | Authors wrote from particular institutions, interests and conventions; absence from a text is not proof of absence |
| Epigraphy, numismatics and inscriptions | Names, titles, chronology, authority, exchange and literacy | Survival and decipherment are uneven; provenance matters |
| Genetics and bioanthropology | Population history, ancestry patterns and biological relationships | Genetic clusters are not ethnic identities; sampling, reference populations and interpretation matter |
| Palaeoclimate and environmental archaeology | Changing rainfall, vegetation, water, soils and human-environment relations | Environmental change does not mechanically determine social outcomes |
| Historical cartography and remote sensing | Changing spatial representations, routes, settlement patterns and land use | Maps encode the purposes and assumptions of their makers; boundaries are not neutral |

### Triangulation

For significant claims, seek independent evidence from more than one method where possible. Triangulation does not mean forcing sources to agree. Contradictions may reveal different periods, social positions, political interests, naming systems or forms of memory.

A source must be described before it is used: author or custodian, date or estimated period, language, location, purpose, method, audience, provenance, access conditions and known limitations.

### Chronology and periodisation

Use dates with stated conventions and uncertainty. Distinguish archaeological periods, dynastic chronologies, oral historical sequences, linguistic reconstructions and colonial administrative periods. Do not treat a period label as a natural boundary shared by all regions.

Avoid using “prehistory” as shorthand for a people without history. Where written records are absent or unavailable, describe the available evidence and its limits rather than implying an absence of historical knowledge.

## 3. Names, peoples and identity

Names must be recorded as historical evidence, not treated as timeless labels.

### Required distinctions

- **Endonym:** a name used by a community for itself, with its language, spelling, pronunciation, period and context recorded where known.
- **Exonym:** a name used by outsiders. Record who used it, when, in what language and for what purpose.
- **Ethnonym:** a name applied to a people or social grouping; its scope can change over time.
- **Toponym:** a place-name. Preserve local forms and document competing names, transliterations and administrative renamings.
- **Political name:** a name for a state, kingdom, confederation, polity or institution. Do not assume it names every resident as one ethnic group.
- **Colonial classification:** a category imposed or formalised by an administrative, missionary, military, scholarly or census system. Record the institution and date.
- **Self-identification:** how people describe themselves in a given context. It may be layered, situational and multiple.

A community may use different names in different languages or contexts. A label can be adopted, rejected, reinterpreted or reclaimed. Do not decide identity on behalf of living communities.

### Naming record fields

Each peoples or place entry should capture:
- preferred current name and community-approved spelling where available;
- historical self-designations and documented variants;
- exonyms and the sources that used them;
- language(s), dialects and the evidence for classification;
- geographic references at an appropriate level of precision;
- time period and historical scope;
- related or neighbouring communities, with relationship type stated rather than presumed;
- source provenance, confidence, disagreement and correction history;
- community permissions, restrictions and attribution requirements.

Do not infer a single ancient identity from a modern name, or a modern ethnic identity from an archaeological assemblage alone.

## 4. Lands, regions and ecological histories

Land is not merely a polygon on a map. It may be a homeland, seasonal range, watershed, pasture, cultivated landscape, sacred place, trade corridor, fishing ground, migration route, jurisdiction or contested territory.

Research should distinguish:
- ecological regions and watersheds;
- lived and named territories;
- seasonal and mobile land use;
- settlement and cultivation zones;
- routes of trade, pilgrimage, migration and pastoral movement;
- historical jurisdictions and political frontiers;
- colonial boundaries and postcolonial state borders;
- contemporary administrative units;
- overlapping customary, statutory and community claims.

Maps must state their date, scale, projection, source, purpose and uncertainty. Do not project current national boundaries backwards as if they existed unchanged in earlier periods. Conversely, do not imply that precolonial boundaries were always fixed or uncontested.

### Mapping principles

1. Preserve local place-names and, where appropriate, multiple names.
2. Mark uncertain locations as approximate rather than assigning false precision.
3. Distinguish a route attested in a source from a route inferred by researchers.
4. Show seasonal movement and networks where a static territorial map would mislead.
5. Record the mapmaker, date, institutional context and intended audience.
6. Treat sensitive sites, sacred geographies and living community locations according to consent and safety requirements.
7. Never publish precise coordinates for vulnerable people, restricted heritage or threatened resources without explicit permission and a risk assessment.

## 5. Population history, mobility and exchange

Population history is a study of movement, continuity, mixture, fission, aggregation, language shift, adoption, displacement and changing forms of belonging. It is not a search for pure or sealed populations.

Migration may be gradual, episodic, seasonal, forced, commercial, political, ecological or multi-generational. Oral accounts of origin can encode social relations and historical change without functioning as modern demographic datasets. Genetic, linguistic, archaeological and oral evidence answer different questions and must not be collapsed into one claim.

Research questions include:
- How did communities adapt to changing water, pasture, soils, disease environments and coastlines?
- How did technologies, crops, livestock and craft practices move?
- How did marriage, adoption, alliance, trade, conflict and incorporation shape communities?
- When did language shift occur, and what evidence supports the proposed chronology?
- How did enslavement, warfare, colonial labour systems and displacement alter population patterns?
- How do contemporary communities narrate ancestry and belonging?

Avoid “replacement” narratives unless the evidence supports a specific demographic process. Avoid treating cultural similarity as proof of direct descent or political unity.

## 6. Political formations and institutions

African political histories include many forms of authority and organisation. The state is one historical form among others, not the universal measure of social complexity.

Research may include:
- kingdoms, empires and territorial states;
- city-states, port cities and commercial polities;
- confederations, federations and shifting alliances;
- chiefdoms and decentralised political systems;
- councils, age-set systems, lineages and community assemblies;
- religious and scholarly institutions;
- guilds, craft networks and merchant diasporas;
- pastoral governance, water and grazing institutions;
- women’s political, economic, spiritual and intellectual authority;
- anti-colonial movements, liberation organisations and postcolonial institutions.

For each formation, investigate its own political vocabulary and institutions. Do not assume that European concepts such as “tribe”, “chief”, “king”, “nation” or “state” translate cleanly into every context. Where a translation is used, retain the original term and explain the limits.

A political history should ask who exercised authority, how it was legitimised, how decisions were made, how resources were governed, how dissent was handled, and how institutions changed.

## 7. Civilisations, cities and regional histories

“Civilisation” is used here as a comparative historical term, not as a hierarchy separating supposedly advanced and backward peoples. It should refer to specific, evidenced social formations and their institutions, technologies, knowledge, arts, settlements and networks.

The following are research pathways, not an exhaustive list or a ranking:

### Nile Valley and North-East Africa
Investigate ancient and later Nile Valley societies, Nubia/Kush, Egypt, Meroë, Aksum and the Horn, Red Sea connections, language histories, religious transformations, agriculture, metallurgy, urban and monumental traditions, and changing political formations. Avoid reducing the Nile Valley to one modern national or racial category.

### Sahara and Sahel
Study the Sahara as a lived and changing region—not an eternal barrier—including pastoral and oasis histories, trans-Saharan routes, urban and scholarly centres, the Ghana/Wagadu, Mali and Songhai formations, Kanem-Bornu, Tuareg histories, and the many communities and political systems that cannot be represented by imperial names alone.

### West Africa and the Atlantic-facing regions
Research diverse histories of the Niger and Senegal basins, forest and coastal societies, Ife, Benin, Oyo, Asante, Dahomey, Fante, Igbo communities and other formations. Treat these as distinct histories with different political structures, languages and chronologies. Study Atlantic commerce and enslavement alongside internal political histories and African agency, without excusing violence or flattening responsibility.

### Central Africa and the Congo basin
Study river systems, forest and savanna ecologies, trade, ironworking, agriculture, mobility, Kongo, Luba, Lunda and other political formations, as well as communities whose histories do not centre on large territorial states. Attend to the diversity of the region and the effects of colonial extraction and boundary-making.

### East Africa, the Great Lakes and the Horn
Research the Swahili coast and Indian Ocean world; inland and coastal trade; Great Lakes kingdoms and communities; Ethiopian and Somali histories; the Rift Valley; pastoral and agropastoral societies; and the many languages and political institutions across the region. Treat “East Africa” as a useful geographic frame, not a single historical unit.

### Southern Africa
Study Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe, Mutapa, Rozvi and other regional formations; mining, cattle economies, agriculture, trade, settlement and mobility; San and Khoe histories; Bantu-language-speaking communities; and the complex histories of colonial conquest, dispossession and resistance. Avoid presenting one group as the sole origin of a landscape.

### Islands and oceanic Africa
Include Madagascar, Zanzibar, Pemba, Comoros, Seychelles, Cabo Verde, São Tomé and Príncipe, Mauritius and other island histories where relevant. Investigate African, Austronesian, Arab, South Asian, European and other connections without treating island societies as peripheral to continental history.

### Diasporas and connected worlds
Study African diasporas as histories of forced displacement, voluntary movement, exchange, cultural creation, political organisation and continuing relationships with African societies. Distinguish different diaspora formations and periods rather than treating them as interchangeable.

## 8. Trade, technology and intellectual exchange

African societies were connected internally and externally through routes and relationships that changed over time. The Sahara, Nile, Red Sea, Mediterranean, Atlantic and Indian Ocean were not simply borders; they were also environments of movement, exchange, encounter and conflict.

Research should document:
- commodities and production systems, including food, livestock, metals, textiles, salt, gold, ivory and other materials;
- merchants, sailors, caravan organisers, craft specialists and intermediaries;
- ports, market towns, river crossings, caravan centres and inland routes;
- currencies, credit, taxation, tribute and commercial law;
- religious, linguistic, artistic and scholarly exchange;
- technologies and their local adaptation;
- coercion, raiding, enslavement and unequal exchange as well as cooperation.

Do not equate trade with peaceful exchange. Do not attribute every innovation to external arrival. Establish the chronology and local evidence for each technology or institution.

## 9. Colonial cartography, borders and classification

Colonial rule did not create all African political boundaries or identities from nothing. It did, however, transform territorial administration, property regimes, labour, censuses, ethnic classification, mobility, legal status and political authority in many regions.

Research must identify the specific colonial power, administrative period, local intermediaries, resistance and negotiation, and the uneven effects of policy. The Berlin Conference and the “Scramble for Africa” are important, but cannot substitute for local histories before, during and after conquest.

For every colonial map or census:
- identify the producing authority and intended use;
- inspect the categories and omissions;
- compare the map with earlier and later evidence;
- distinguish surveyed boundaries from claimed or schematic ones;
- document how local names and political institutions were translated or suppressed;
- avoid treating the colonial archive as a neutral description of society.

Postcolonial borders and state institutions also have histories of negotiation, conflict, reform and public meaning. Contemporary African identities cannot be reduced to colonial imposition.

## 10. Regional case pathway: Turkana, Ateker and the Lake Turkana region

This is a priority research pathway because it connects the corpus's Turkana-rooted context with broader questions of land, pastoralism, oral history, language, political organisation and historical method. It must not become a template imposed on other communities.

### Scope and distinctions

- Treat Turkana as a distinct community with its own histories, language, institutions and contemporary self-understandings.
- “Ateker” is a wider relational and linguistic-historical frame; do not use it as a synonym for Turkana or imply that all communities grouped under it share one identity.
- Record the source and context for every proposed relationship among Turkana, Jie, Karimojong, Iteso, Toposa, Nyangatom and other neighbouring communities.
- Distinguish community self-understanding from classifications in linguistic, colonial or academic literature.

### Research themes

1. Lake Turkana, Omo-Turkana basin, escarpments, rivers, seasonal ranges and ecological change.
2. Pastoral systems, livestock histories, herd management, water and pasture knowledge.
3. Oral traditions of origin, migration, incorporation and political change, preserving multiple versions.
4. Trade and exchange, including grain, iron, livestock and connections with neighbouring communities.
5. Diviners, elders, age-set institutions, women’s knowledge and other forms of authority.
6. Colonial encounters, resistance, administrative reorganisation and border-making.
7. Contemporary land rights, education, livelihoods, conservation, development and community archives.

John Lamphear’s work is a useful starting point because it reads Turkana oral traditions alongside archaeology and linguistics, and explicitly cautions against a single, monolithic origin narrative. Paul Lane’s review of East African pastoral archaeology offers a longer regional timescale and highlights the need to combine archaeology with palaeoenvironmental, linguistic and population evidence. These works are starting points for critical reading, not substitutes for community-led research.

## 12. Evidence and editorial rules

Use the corpus evidence labels consistently:

- **VERIFIED:** directly supported by a reliable primary record or robust, convergent evidence.
- **CORROBORATED:** supported by multiple credible sources, with remaining limits stated.
- **DISPUTED:** credible sources or communities offer materially different accounts.
- **UNVERIFIED:** a claim is recorded but has not been adequately checked.
- **INTERPRETATION:** an explicitly reasoned reading of evidence.
- **OPINION:** a value judgement or personal position.
- **TESTIMONY:** a person's or community's account, attributed and contextualised.
- **PROPOSAL:** a suggested future research or documentation action.

Additional rules:
1. Separate fact, interpretation, testimony and political argument.
2. Cite sources close to the claims they support.
3. Prefer primary sources for the specific question, while critically assessing their provenance and purpose.
4. Do not cite a source merely because it repeats a claim; trace the evidence where possible.
5. Preserve uncertainty and disagreement instead of manufacturing consensus.
6. Do not infer racial identity, ethnicity or language from appearance, objects or genetics alone.
7. Do not describe communities as static survivals of an earlier age.
8. Use respectful, community-preferred terminology and correct errors when notified.
9. Mark AI-generated summaries as aids, never as sources or independent verification.
10. Record research gaps openly; no fieldwork, interview or community approval may be claimed unless it occurred and is documented.

## 13. Ethics, custody and rights

Community knowledge is not an unowned dataset. Researchers must establish who has authority to share a story, name, image, map, song, sacred account, genealogy, site location or other knowledge—and whether that authority is individual, collective, institutional or contested.

Minimum safeguards:
- prior and ongoing informed consent for interviews and recordings;
- clear explanation of purpose, storage, access, publication and reuse;
- permission for translation, transcription, quotation and derivative works;
- attribution at the level requested by contributors;
- options for anonymity, restricted access, embargo or withdrawal where feasible;
- respect for collective custodianship and community protocols;
- benefit-sharing and accessible copies of outputs;
- no extraction for model training or commercial reuse without explicit permission;
- secure handling of sensitive locations and personal data;
- a correction and takedown process.

Public availability does not automatically mean ethical permission to republish. Copyright, customary authority, privacy, cultural protocols and safety must be considered separately.

## 14. Initial source map and bibliography

The following sources are research entry points. They are not a substitute for reading the works in full, checking their methods, and adding community-held and primary sources. Where a source is a broad synthesis, use it to locate more specific evidence.

### Continental and regional synthesis
- UNESCO. *General History of Africa*, Volumes I–X. The series offers a large, collaborative historical synthesis with volumes on prehistory, ancient civilisations, regional periods, colonial rule and diasporas. Read critically and consult the bibliographies and regional chapters.
  - https://www.unesco.org/en/general-history-africa
- Musau, Zipporah. “UNESCO’s work on the General History of Africa helps restore forgotten history.” *Africa Renewal*, 17 March 2026. Useful overview of the project and the place of oral traditions in historical recovery.
  - https://africarenewal.un.org/en/magazine/unescos-work-general-history-africa-helps-restore-forgotten-history

### Archaeology, pastoralism and population history
- Lane, Paul J. “The Archaeology of Pastoralism and Stock-Keeping in East Africa.” In *The Oxford Handbook of African Archaeology* (2013), pp. 585–601. Regional synthesis on pastoralism, settlement and archaeological approaches.
  - https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780199569885.013.0040
- Dueppen, Stephen A. “The Archaeology of West Africa, ca. 800 BCE to 1500 CE.” *History Compass* 14, no. 6 (2016): 247–263. Reviews archaeological evidence and the diversity of West African trajectories.
  - https://doi.org/10.1111/hic3.12316

### Turkana, Ateker and East African histories
- Lamphear, John. “The People of the Grey Bull: The Origin and Expansion of the Turkana.” *The Journal of African History* 29, no. 1 (1988): 27–39. Reads oral traditions alongside archaeology and linguistics; emphasises complex, multiple processes rather than one origin.
  - https://doi.org/10.1017/S0021853700035970
- Lamphear, John. *The Scattering Time: Turkana Responses to Colonial Rule*. Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1992. A detailed historical study of Turkana encounters with colonial administration and resistance.
  - https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198202264.001.0001
- “Turkana-Dassanech Relations: Economic Diversification and Inter-Communal Conflicts, 1984–2015.” University of Nairobi repository thesis. Use as a research lead and assess its oral-history citations and framing against other sources and community perspectives.
  - https://erepository.uonbi.ac.ke/server/api/core/bitstreams/f75c719a-5713-4b91-819a-18f50a1024bb/content

### Indian Ocean and connected worlds
- Library of Congress. “Africa in the Indian Ocean World: Introduction.” A guide to resources on East African participation in long-distance oceanic networks.
  - https://guides.loc.gov/africa-in-the-indian-ocean-world

### Further source acquisition priorities
- African and local-language manuscripts, inscriptions and archival catalogues.
- National and regional archives, with attention to provenance and colonial administrative bias.
- Archaeological site reports and museum catalogues with documented provenance.
- Community historians, cultural institutions, language organisations and local researchers.
- Historical maps from African institutions and local archives, not only European map collections.
- Research on women’s histories, labour, craft, agriculture, ecology and everyday life, which can be underrepresented in state-centred narratives.

8. Link each future claim to a source record and evidence label.

## Editorial principle

**The continent is not one people, and plurality is not fragmentation.** Historical connections do not erase distinct communities; distinct communities do not exist outside exchange and relationship. The work is to document both difference and connection with precision, evidence and respect.
