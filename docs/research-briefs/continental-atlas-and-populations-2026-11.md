## Purpose

Summarise the regional archaeological and population-genetic picture of Africa from the Late Pleistocene through the African Humid Period and into the later Holocene, as background for the Before Africa programme.

## Core findings

1. **Multiple parallel histories.** Africa’s prehistory is regional, not unitary. Connections and exchanges existed, but so did long periods of relative isolation and independent development.

2. **Green Sahara as one chapter.** The African Humid Period (~14,500–5,000 BP) created lakes, wetlands and grasslands across large parts of what is now desert. Communities fished, hunted, gathered, made pottery and, later in some regions, herded livestock. Aridification after ~5,000 BP prompted adaptation and settlement shift, not simple disappearance.

3. **Cultural diffusion of pastoralism.** The 2025 Takarkori genomes indicate that pastoralism in the central Sahara spread into a deeply divergent, largely isolated North African lineage related to earlier Iberomaurusian (Taforalt) foragers. This supports cultural transmission rather than large-scale demographic replacement in that locality and time slice.

4. **Deep southern African continuity.** Ancient genomes from southern Africa document substantial genetic continuity over at least 9,000 years in sampled populations, together with deep differentiation from other African groups.

5. **Pottery before farming in some regions.** Early Holocene pottery at Ounjougou (Mali) and related Saharan/West African sequences shows ceramic technology associated with hunter-gatherer economies exploiting wild grasses, independent of settled agriculture.

6. **No single continental self-name.** Attested names (Kemet, Kush, Punt, Libu, Afri, Greek Libya/Aithiopes) are regional or external. No primary evidence establishes a universal Indigenous continental name before Roman provincial “Africa.”

## Population continuity types (must be distinguished)

- Biological continuity (genetic relationships)
- Cultural continuity (practices and technologies)
- Linguistic continuity (language transmission)
- Geographical continuity (persistence in or return to landscapes)

These do not necessarily coincide.

## Research priorities

- Expand ancient DNA sampling (Central Africa, additional Saharan and West African Holocene sites).
- Integrate morphometric, isotopic and proteomic data with genomic results.
- Refine archaeolinguistic correlations with tighter chronological control.
- Document local environmental knowledge systems as independent historical records.
- Maintain African scholarly and community leadership in sampling, interpretation and benefit-sharing.

### Verified population-genetics updates

- **Takarkori:** Salem et al. (2025), *Nature* 641, 144–150, DOI 10.1038/s41586-025-08793-7. Two women from southwestern Libya, directly dated to approximately 7,158–6,281 calibrated BP. The preferred admixture graph models ~93% ancestry from a previously unsampled North African lineage and ~7% from a deeply ancient Levantine-related source. These are model-dependent proportions, not ethnic identities. The result supports limited substantial sub-Saharan-to-northern gene flow in the sampled individuals and period, not an absence of all contact across the Sahara.
- **Southern Africa:** Jakobsson et al. (2026 issue; online 3 December 2025), *Nature* 650, 156–163, DOI 10.1038/s41586-025-09811-4. Twenty-eight individuals from south of the Limpopo River, dated ~10,200–150 cal BP. Little spatiotemporal stratification is reported in this sample across ~9,000 years. The study is not a census of all southern African peoples; modern Khoe-San ancestry profiles are varied and include later admixture.
- **Eastern and south-central African foragers:** Lipson et al. (2022), *Nature* 603, 290–296. Six new ancient genomes spanning ~18,000 years are analysed with other published individuals. Modelled ancestry sources are proxies, not prehistoric ethnic groups.
- **Bantu-speaking expansion:** the 2024 *Nature* study includes 1,763 participants from 147 populations in 14 countries and ancient DNA. Bantu is a linguistic grouping; the demographic history includes admixture and regional variation, not a biological race or uniform replacement.

### Interpretation safeguards

1. Genetic continuity is not cultural, linguistic or political stasis.
2. A modelled ancestry proportion is not a direct measurement of ethnicity, skin colour or self-identification.
3. A small ancient-DNA sample cannot represent an entire region or modern population.
4. “No detectable signal” is bounded by sample coverage, reference panels, methods and statistical power.
5. Archaeological cultures, language families and genetic clusters must not be mapped one-to-one.

### Regional materials acquisition priorities

For each region, assemble site reports, stratigraphy, direct dating, artefact catalogues, environmental records, relevant genomes and supplementary data, language histories, oral-history protocols and heritage governance documentation. Prioritise Central African, additional Saharan, West African and eastern African sampling gaps. Do not publish restricted coordinates, genomic data, sacred knowledge or community-held recordings without authority.
