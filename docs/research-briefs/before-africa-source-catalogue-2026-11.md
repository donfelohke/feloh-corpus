# Before Africa — Source Catalogue, Materials and Research Resources

This catalogue records traceable scholarly and institutional resources for the Before Africa programme. It is a starting bibliography, not a claim of exhaustive coverage. Prefer primary research for specific findings; use institutional and synthesis resources for context. Check publication dates, errata, supplementary data, access conditions and later corrections before citing.

## 1. Human origins, fossils and deep time

1. **Harmand, S. et al. (2015). “3.3-million-year-old stone tools from Lomekwi 3, West Turkana, Kenya.” _Nature_ 521, 310–315.**  
   DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/nature14464  
   Primary report of the Lomekwian assemblage. Supports stone-tool manufacture by hominins before the currently known emergence of Homo; the makers are not identified as Homo sapiens.

2. **Hublin, J.-J. et al. (2017). “New fossils from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco and the pan-African origin of Homo sapiens.” _Nature_ 546, 289–292.**  
   https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22336  
   Fossil morphology and associated chronology (~315 ± 34 ka) support a geographically distributed African evolutionary process. Do not turn “pan-African” into a claim that all regions contributed equally or that a single population was uniform.

3. **Richter, D. et al. (2017). “The age of the hominin fossils from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco, and the origins of the Middle Stone Age.” _Nature_ 546, 293–296.**  
   https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22335  
   Dating study for the Jebel Irhoud archaeological and fossil context.

4. **Oppenheimer, C. et al. (2022). “The age of the oldest known Homo sapiens from eastern Africa.” _Nature_ 601, 579–583.**  
   https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04275-8  
   Revises the minimum age of Omo I in Ethiopia to 233 ± 22 ka. This is a minimum age, not an exact birth date for the species.

5. **Grine, F. E. et al. (2007). “Late Pleistocene human skull from Hofmeyr, South Africa, and modern human origins.” _Science_ 315, 226–229.**  
   DOI: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1136294  
   Dates and compares the Hofmeyr cranium (~36.2 ± 3.3 ka). Morphometric affinities are not racial classifications and should not be read as proof of a single migration route.

## 2. Middle Stone Age technologies, symbolic practices and exchange

6. **Brooks, A. S. et al. (2018). “Long-distance stone transport and pigment use in the earliest Middle Stone Age.” _Science_ 360, 90–94.**  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29545508/  
   Olorgesailie Basin, Kenya: prepared cores, points, red pigment and non-local stone procured from at least 25–50 km away, in contexts dated approximately 320–295 ka.

7. **Smithsonian Human Origins Program. “Evolution of Human Innovation.”**  
   https://humanorigins.si.edu/research/east-african-research-projects/evolution-human-innovation  
   Accessible institutional overview of the Olorgesailie research programme and its archaeological context.

8. **Henshilwood, C. S. et al. (2002). “Emergence of modern human behavior: Middle Stone Age engravings from South Africa.” _Science_ 295, 1278–1280.**  
   DOI: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1067575  
   Reports engraved ochre from Blombos Cave, with a mean layer date around 77 ka.

9. **Henshilwood, C. S. et al. (2009). “Engraved ochres from the Middle Stone Age levels at Blombos Cave, South Africa.” _Journal of Human Evolution_.**  
   DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhevol.2009.01.005  
   Reports additional deliberately incised ochre from ~75–100 ka contexts. Interpret symbolic behaviour through the full archaeological context, not isolated objects alone.

10. **UNESCO World Heritage Centre. “Tassili n’Ajjer.”**  
    https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/179  
    Institutional description of the Algerian plateau, its rock art, archaeological remains and environmental sequence. Rock art images require contextual dating and interpretation; they are not literal photographs of one unchanging population.

## 3. African Humid Period, Sahara and Holocene lifeways

11. **Salem, N. et al. (2025). “Ancient DNA from the Green Sahara reveals ancestral North African lineage.” _Nature_ 641, 144–150.**  
    https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08793-7  
    Two directly dated female individuals from Takarkori, southwestern Libya (~7,158–6,281 cal BP). The authors’ preferred graph models ~93% ancestry from a previously unsampled North African lineage and ~7% from a deeply ancient Levantine-related source. Their analyses find limited evidence of substantial sub-Saharan-to-northern gene flow in the studied individuals and period. The study supports cultural diffusion of pastoralism in this locality; it does not represent all Saharan populations or all directions and episodes of contact.

12. **Sereno, P. C. et al. (2008). “Lakeside Cemeteries in the Sahara: 5000 Years of Holocene Population and Environmental Change.” _PLoS ONE_ 3(8): e2995.**  
    https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0002995  
    Gobero, Niger: cemetery and environmental evidence for changing Holocene communities around a palaeolake.

13. **Huysecom, E. et al. (2009). “The emergence of pottery in Africa during the tenth millennium cal BC: new evidence from Ounjougou (Mali).” _Antiquity_ 83, 905–917.**  
    https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003598X00099245  
    Reports ceramics before 9400 cal BC in an archaeological context associated with wild-grass exploitation. Important evidence that pottery need not depend on settled agriculture.

14. **Huysecom, E. (2020). “The First Emergence of Ceramic Production in Africa.” _Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Anthropology_.**  
    https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780190854584.013.66  
    Regional synthesis of early African ceramic production and the Ounjougou sequence.

15. **NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information. “End of the African Humid Period.”**  
    https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/2021-11/5%20End%20of%20the%20Africian%20Humid%20Period%20-Final_OCT%202021.pdf  
    Accessible palaeoclimate synthesis. The AHP’s timing and termination varied regionally; orbital forcing, monsoon dynamics, vegetation and dust feedbacks should be considered together.

## 4. Ancient population history and genetics

16. **Jakobsson, M. et al. (2026 issue; published online 3 December 2025). “Homo sapiens-specific evolution unveiled by ancient southern African genomes.” _Nature_ 650, 156–163.**  
    https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09811-4  
    DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09811-4  
    Whole-genome data from 28 ancient individuals south of the Limpopo River, dated ~10,200–150 cal BP. The sampled individuals show little spatiotemporal stratification across ~9,000 years; ancient individuals older than ~1,400 cal BP fall outside the range of modern comparison variation. The paper estimates substantial ancient southern African ancestry in some present-day Khoe-San groups. Sampling is geographically and temporally uneven; this is not a claim that all southern African communities were one unchanged population.

17. **Lipson, M. et al. (2022). “Ancient DNA and deep population structure in sub-Saharan African foragers.” _Nature_ 603, 290–296.**  
    https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04430-9  
    Six new ancient genomes from eastern and south-central Africa, spanning ~18,000 years, analysed with other published ancient individuals. Statistical ancestry sources are model-dependent proxies, not prehistoric ethnic or tribal names.

18. **Fregel, R. et al. (2018). “Ancient genomes from North Africa evidence prehistoric migrations to the Maghreb from both the Levant and Europe.” _PNAS_ 115, 6774–6779.**  
    https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1800851115  
    Ancient genomes from Moroccan Neolithic contexts and southern Iberia. Useful for comparing local continuity, adoption of farming practices and later admixture; do not collapse all Maghrebi periods into one ancestry profile.

19. **“The genetic legacy of the expansion of Bantu-speaking peoples in Africa.” (2024). _Nature_ 625, 540–547.**  
    https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06770-6  
    Large genomic study including 1,763 participants from 147 populations in 14 countries and ancient DNA. The Bantu expansion is a linguistic-demographic process with admixture and regional variation, not a biological race or simple continent-wide replacement.

20. **van de Loosdrecht, M. et al. (2018). “Pleistocene North African genomes link Near Eastern and sub-Saharan African human populations.” _Science_ 360, 548–552.**  
    https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aar8380  
    Reports genome-wide data from seven ~15,000-year-old individuals associated with the Iberomaurusian culture at Taforalt, Morocco. The original analysis modelled a component using available sub-Saharan reference populations. Salem et al. (2025) subsequently tested Takarkori as a North African proxy and obtained a better-fitting model for the Taforalt-related component. These are successive model interpretations, not evidence that one ancient population was a modern racial or ethnic category.

21. **Fregel, R. et al. (2018). “Ancient genomes from North Africa evidence prehistoric migrations to the Maghreb from both the Levant and Europe.” _PNAS_ 115, 6774–6779.**  
    https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1800851115  
    Ancient genomes from Moroccan Neolithic contexts and southern Iberia; useful for studying local continuity, agricultural adoption and later admixture. Do not collapse the Maghreb’s different prehistoric periods into one ancestry profile.

## 5. Languages, oral history and historical method

22. **Vossen, R. & Dimmendaal, G. J. (eds.) (2020). _The Oxford Handbook of African Languages_. Oxford University Press.**  
    https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780199609895.001.0001  
    Major reference for African language classification, historical linguistics, contact, sociolinguistics and orature. Treat Nilo-Saharan classification as debated and avoid treating “Khoisan” as a single established language family.

23. **Mobley, C. (2018). “Documentary Sources and Methods for Precolonial African History.” _Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History_.**  
    https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780190277734.013.150  
    Methodological guidance on reading written records critically and triangulating documentary evidence with archaeology, linguistics, ethnography, oral tradition, genetics and environmental history.

24. **Belcher, S. (2018). “Oral Traditions as Sources.” _Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History_.**  
    https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780190277734.013.363  
    Methodological introduction to the collection, analysis and presentation of oral traditions as historical sources.

25. **UNESCO. _General History of Africa_ (Volumes I–XI collection).**  
    https://www.unesco.org/en/general-history-africa?hub=180364  
    Major African-centred scholarly collection. Use individual volume chapters and bibliographies rather than citing the series generically.

26. **Harvard African Language Program. “Introduction to African Languages.”**  
    https://alp.fas.harvard.edu/introduction-african-languages  
    Introductory resource only. For disputed higher-level classifications, use specialist comparative-linguistic scholarship such as the Oxford Handbook.

## 6. Research ethics, heritage authority and governance

27. **Gibbon, V. E. (2020). “African ancient DNA research requires robust ethics and permission protocols.” _Nature Reviews Genetics_ 21, 645–647.**  
    https://www.nature.com/articles/s41576-020-00285-w  
    Calls attention to the gap between ethics and permission requirements for research on living people and ancient human remains.

28. **“Charting a landmark-driven path forward for population genetics and ancient DNA research in Africa.” (2024). _The American Journal of Human Genetics_ 111, 1243–1251.**  
    https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajhg.2024.05.019  
    Argues for African scholarly leadership, local institutional capacity and community partnership in African population-genetics and ancient-DNA research.

29. **UNESCO World Heritage Centre — World Heritage List and state-of-conservation resources.**  
    https://whc.unesco.org/  
    Use site-specific records for heritage status, threats and conservation context. A World Heritage listing is not a substitute for local community consultation or national heritage law.

## 7. Research materials and data to collect

For each archaeological site or population-history case, create a source packet containing:

- **Publication record:** full citation, DOI, stable URL, publication/version dates, correction history and access date.
- **Supplementary materials:** supplementary PDF, tables, methods, radiocarbon determinations, sample metadata and data availability statement.
- **Site record:** site name and alternate spellings, country/region, coordinates only where public and appropriate, excavation history, stratigraphy and custodianship.
- **Chronology:** calibrated dates, probability ranges, dating method, laboratory code and association between dated material and the archaeological claim.
- **Material culture:** excavation reports, artefact catalogues, images with rights/provenance, archaeobotany, zooarchaeology, lithics, ceramics and residue analysis.
- **Ancient DNA:** sample identifier, skeletal element, direct/indirect date, coverage, contamination assessment, capture/sequencing method, comparison panel, statistical model, code/data accession and ethical permissions.
- **Environment:** pollen, phytoliths, sediment cores, lake levels, faunal records, palaeohydrology and climate-model assumptions.
- **Language and oral history:** language and dialect, original wording, recording/transcription details, translator, speaker or knowledge-holder permissions, genre, date and community restrictions.
- **Governance:** national permits, institutional approvals, community consultation, data-use terms, repatriation/custodianship issues and benefit-sharing arrangements.

Do not mirror restricted datasets, human genomic data, sacred knowledge or community-held recordings into public repositories without explicit authority. Link to controlled-access repositories and describe access requirements instead.

## 8. Priority gaps and acquisition queue

1. **Central Africa:** ancient DNA and archaeological sampling gaps; expand coverage without treating rainforest communities as a single population.
2. **West Africa:** integrate Ounjougou, Bosumpra, Nok and regional archaeological sequences with careful chronology; avoid assuming pottery, language and ancestry map together.
3. **Eastern Africa:** expand comparative work around Lake Turkana, the Rift, the Horn and the Great Lakes; distinguish early foragers from later pastoralist and farmer movements.
4. **Sahara:** more genomes from different periods, sites and ecological zones; test contact in both directions and across multiple humid phases.
5. **North Africa:** compare Taforalt, Ifri Ouberrid, Ifri n’Amr o’Moussa, Takarkori, Nile Valley and later Maghrebi populations without treating “North African” as one timeless group.
6. **Southern Africa:** broaden sampling beyond existing sites and integrate archaeology, oral history, skeletal anthropology and community-led interpretation.
7. **Language histories:** commission specialist reviews of Afroasiatic, Niger-Congo, Nilo-Saharan proposals, Khoe-Kwadi, Kx’a, Tuu, Hadza and Sandawe; identify where classification is contested.
8. **Material and intellectual heritage:** add African-authored specialist studies on medicine, metallurgy, agriculture, architecture, astronomy, navigation, law, philosophy, music and craft.

## 9. Citation and evidence rules

- Cite the primary paper for a specific empirical result; cite a review for synthesis.
- Record exact sample size, date range, geographical scope and uncertainty.
- Distinguish direct observation from the authors’ model and from later interpretation.
- Never infer skin colour, language, ethnicity or modern racial identity directly from a genetic component or archaeological culture.
- Do not describe “no detectable signal” as proof that an event never occurred.
- Treat ancient DNA as one evidence stream, not a replacement for archaeology, linguistics, oral history or community knowledge.
- Mark access date and preserve stable DOI links. Verify that links resolve before adding new entries.
