# BLACKS: THE DOCS & DATA
## Black identity, African plurality, cultural affirmation and self-determination

**Programme:** FELOH Sovereign Corpus  
**Status:** RESEARCH FRAMEWORK · living programme  
**Established:** 17 November 2026  
**Canonical home:** FELOH Corpus

> **Foundational statement:** “Black = Beautiful” is a statement of cultural affirmation, historical reclamation and self-determination.

> **Historical proposition:** The peoples and lands now grouped under the continental designation “Africa” have histories that precede the dominance of that designation. They were never one undifferentiated people or civilisation. They are a mosaic of peoples, languages, places, political formations, knowledge traditions, artistic practices and intellectual lineages.

This programme documents Black life and knowledge without flattening African plurality, projecting modern racial categories unchanged into antiquity, or treating external recognition as the source of African worth.

---

## 1. Purpose

BLACKS: THE DOCS & DATA is a long-term research, documentation and preservation programme. It brings together historical evidence, indigenous and community knowledge, intellectual traditions, cultural expression, contemporary Black experience and the institutions required to preserve and develop that knowledge.

The programme asks:

- Who were the peoples, communities and societies later grouped under the name Africa?
- What names did they use for themselves, their lands, languages, institutions and neighbours?
- How did names and classifications change through trade, migration, conquest, religious exchange, enslavement, colonial rule, independence and contemporary self-identification?
- What forms of knowledge did different societies develop, preserve, test and transmit?
- Which sages, philosophers, teachers, artists, scientists, builders, healers, organisers and knowledge custodians belong to particular intellectual histories?
- How have racial hierarchies shaped the representation of Black bodies, hair, skin, beauty, intelligence and cultural achievement?
- How have Black communities reclaimed identity and created institutions, enterprises and futures on their own terms?
- What must be preserved, by whom, under what rights and with whose consent?

The programme is not a claim that every subject can be reduced to race. It studies the histories and contemporary meanings of Blackness while retaining the distinct histories of the peoples, places and traditions involved.

## 2. The foundational distinction: peoples before labels

Peoples, places, languages, political communities and knowledge traditions existed before a single continental label became dominant. Their histories must not be made to begin with the arrival of an outsider, the appearance of a name in a foreign text, or the creation of a colonial map.

The word **Africa** has a layered history:

1. Ancient Greek geographical writing used **Libya** for broad parts of the lands south of the Mediterranean, with scope varying by author and period.
2. Roman usage of **Africa** is attested for a region associated with Carthage and Roman North Africa, especially after the Roman defeat of Carthage in 146 BCE. The exact etymology remains disputed.
3. Related terms, including **Ifriqiya**, developed in later North African and Arabic historical-geographical contexts.
4. The use of **Africa** as the name for the entire continental landmass became dominant through a long process of geographical writing and cartography, especially in early modern Europe.
5. European conquest, colonial administration, racial classification and border-making subsequently gave the continental label additional political and administrative force.
6. In the present, **African** is also a meaningful identity claimed and shaped by people across the continent and its diasporas. Its contemporary self-identification cannot be reduced to its earlier external uses.

**Editorial correction:** Do not state that European colonial invaders invented the word Africa. Its attested history is older and more complex. The defensible argument is that external powers expanded, standardised and institutionalised geographical, racial and administrative categories, often overriding or subordinating local names, political boundaries and self-understandings.

The research question is therefore not simply “What was Africa really called?” It is:

**Who named which people or place, in which language, at what time, for what territory, in which source, and under what conditions of power?**

### 2.1 Naming vocabulary

| Term | Working meaning | Research requirement |
|---|---|---|
| **Endonym** | Name used by a people for themselves or their community | Record language, speaker/community and historical period |
| **Exonym** | Name used by others for a people or place | Identify who used it, why, and whether it was accepted or contested |
| **Ethnonym** | Name applied to a people or group | Do not assume it maps neatly onto one language, ancestry or political identity |
| **Toponym** | Place or geographical name | Record local forms, historical forms, translations and changes |
| **Autonym** | Name used by a group for itself, often in linguistic or ethnographic description | Verify community usage and context |
| **Colonial classification** | Administrative or scholarly category created or formalised under colonial power | Identify the institution, purpose, date and consequences |
| **Self-identification** | Identity claimed by people themselves | Preserve contemporary agency; do not treat identity as fixed across all eras |

Names can be adopted, rejected, translated, imposed, reclaimed or repurposed. A term's origin does not permanently determine what it means to people who later claim it.

### 2.2 The mosaic principle

There was no single precolonial African language, government, religion, philosophy, aesthetic or political system. The continent's history includes large states and empires, city-states, pastoral societies, trading networks, village institutions, councils, federations, mobile communities and many other forms of collective life.

The mosaic is not a claim of isolation. It includes exchange, intermarriage, migration, conflict, alliance, borrowing, religious change and shared intellectual networks. Difference and connection must be documented together.

## 3. Blackness: historical identity and analytical care

**Black** has been used in different times and places as a colour description, a racial classification, a social position, a political identity, a cultural affiliation and a chosen language of solidarity. Its meanings are historically situated.

The programme distinguishes:

- ancient African populations and their own identities;
- historical names recorded by neighbouring societies, traders, chroniclers and states;
- racial categories developed and institutionalised through slavery, colonialism and modern racial regimes;
- Black identities formed through resistance, political organising, cultural movements and diaspora experience;
- contemporary self-identification by people and communities.

Do not project a single modern racial identity backwards onto all ancient populations. An ancient Egyptian, Kushite, Aksumite, Amazigh, Yoruba, Turkana, Swahili, Khoe-San or other person should first be studied through the evidence and categories relevant to their own time and society. Present-day Black communities may have meaningful relationships to these histories, but that relationship is not a licence to erase historical distinctions.

**Working principle:** The heritage now studied and celebrated by Black communities is older and more diverse than the racial systems that later attempted to classify, rank and govern people.

## 4. BLACK = BEAUTIFUL

“Black = Beautiful” is a statement of cultural affirmation, historical reclamation and self-determination.

### 4.1 Cultural affirmation

Affirming the dignity, humanity, creativity, intellectual capacity, beauty and worth of Black people. This includes recognising diverse skin tones, facial features, body forms, hair textures, languages, histories and expressions without establishing a new compulsory standard.

### 4.2 Historical reclamation

Recovering, documenting and contextualising histories, names, knowledge systems, artistic practices, intellectual traditions and community memories that have been marginalised, misrepresented, appropriated or left out of dominant narratives.

### 4.3 Self-determination

The agency of people and communities to define identities, preserve knowledge, govern cultural representation, build institutions, own enterprises, establish research priorities and shape their futures.

### 4.4 Historical movement and context

The phrase **Black Is Beautiful** became a prominent cultural and political affirmation in the United States during the 1960s and 1970s, in relation to Black Power, natural-hair politics, Black arts, independent media and the broader struggle against racialised standards of appearance.

The movement had important precursors in earlier Black organising, intellectual life, print culture, beauty enterprises and Pan-African thought. Harlem's African Jazz-Art Society & Studios (AJASS), the Grandassa Models and photographer Kwame Brathwaite are important subjects for documenting the visual, entrepreneurial and community-organising dimensions of the movement. The 1962 Naturally '62 fashion event is a significant case study; do not reduce the entire movement to a single event or claim an uncontested sole origin for the phrase.

The movement's history must also include its tensions: gender, class, colourism, commercialisation, the policing of supposedly authentic Black appearance, and the difference between cultural visibility and economic ownership.

**The principle is affirmation, not aesthetic coercion.** No hairstyle, skin tone, body type, dress or cultural practice should become a new test of whether someone is authentically Black.

## 5. Research domains

### A. Peoples, lands and self-naming
Endonyms, exonyms, ethnonyms, toponyms, language names, clan and lineage names, titles, political identities, historical geography, migration, settlement and boundary change.

### B. African historical mosaics
Regional and local histories across the Maghreb, Nile Valley, Sahara, Sahel, West Africa, Central Africa, East Africa, the Horn, Great Lakes, Southern Africa, islands and diasporic routes. Use historical regions and networks where modern national borders would distort the subject.

### C. Sages and intellectual traditions
Philosophers, teachers, scribes, jurists, theologians, poets, scientists, healers, astronomers, mathematicians, architects, engineers, navigators, artists, historians, oral custodians and community knowledge holders.

Examples for scoped research include:
- **Ptahhotep** and the textual tradition of ancient Egyptian instruction and ethical reflection;
- **Zera Yacob** and the seventeenth-century Ethiopian philosophical tradition;
- **Ahmed Baba** and the scholarly networks of Timbuktu and the Songhay-era Sahel;
- **Augustine of Hippo**, studied within the religious, linguistic and political worlds of Roman North Africa;
- **Henry Odera Oruka** and the modern project of documenting philosophical sages in African communities.

These examples belong to different eras, languages, intellectual settings and genres. They do not represent one continuous school or a single shared doctrine. Research must identify each person's sources, intellectual context, works, transmission and scholarly debate.

### D. Knowledge systems and technologies
Ecology, pastoralism, agriculture, medicine, pharmacology, metallurgy, ceramics, textiles, architecture, engineering, mathematics, astronomy, navigation, trade, music, movement, law, education, oral literature and systems of observation and experimentation.

### E. Identity, consciousness and liberation
Pan-Africanism, Negritude, Black consciousness, Black Power, anti-colonial movements, decolonisation, diaspora, feminism, labour, civil rights, political organising, cultural nationalism and debates about solidarity and difference.

### F. Beauty, embodiment and cultural expression
Skin, hair, locs, adornment, clothing, visual arts, photography, music, dance, movement, language, foodways and body practices. Study both historical context and contemporary agency. Do not treat a present-day practice as unchanged evidence of an ancient custom.

### G. Coloniality, classification and political economy
Conquest, enslavement, land alienation, missionary and colonial education, racial science, censuses, ethnography, borders, labour regimes, resource extraction, museums, archives, intellectual property, cultural appropriation, colourism and texturism.

### H. Institutions, ownership and sovereign futures
Schools, libraries, archives, museums, research institutes, community organisations, publishers, media, enterprises, technology infrastructure, digital preservation, language revitalisation, repatriation and community stewardship.

### I. Black life and diaspora
The histories and contemporary experiences of African-descended communities across the world, including displacement, enslavement, resistance, cultural continuity, adaptation, self-naming, mixed identities and new forms of belonging. Do not collapse distinct diasporas into one story.

## 6. Research method and evidence discipline

This programme follows the corpus's [Evidence and Editorial Standards](../EVIDENCE-AND-EDITORIAL-STANDARDS.md).

Every substantive claim must be scoped and statused. Use the existing labels:

- **VERIFIED** — inspectable evidence supports the claim.
- **CORROBORATED** — multiple genuinely independent sources support it.
- **DISPUTED** — credible evidence or interpretations conflict.
- **UNVERIFIED** — encountered but not adequately checked.
- **INTERPRETATION** — reasoned analysis based on identified evidence.
- **OPINION** — attributed viewpoint or value judgement.
- **TESTIMONY** — contextualised first-person or community account.
- **PROPOSAL** — future research, policy or action.

For each research record, capture as applicable:

1. Claim, question or subject.
2. People, place, language and period.
3. Original name(s), spelling, transliteration and pronunciation where documented.
4. Source type, author/creator, date, provenance and access conditions.
5. Primary evidence and relevant secondary scholarship.
6. Translation decisions and uncertainty.
7. Competing explanations and counterevidence.
8. Community attribution, consent and restrictions.
9. Claim status and rationale.
10. Related corpus records and specialist repositories.

### 6.1 Source hierarchy is question-dependent

Prefer primary evidence where it exists and is appropriate: inscriptions, manuscripts, archival records, material culture, archaeological context, linguistic data, oral testimony, community-held records and contemporaneous documentation. Primary does not automatically mean neutral or complete. Secondary scholarship is essential for context, dating, interpretation and debate.

A written colonial record is evidence of what its author or institution recorded; it is not automatically a transparent account of the people described. Oral history is evidence and intellectual work; it requires attribution, context, consent and careful interpretation, not automatic dismissal or automatic factual promotion.

### 6.2 Community knowledge and research ethics

- Seek permission before recording or publishing restricted, sacred, private or collectively held knowledge.
- Attribute knowledge to its custodians and communities where safe and appropriate.
- Preserve original-language terms and community-preferred spellings where possible.
- Do not invent translations, etymologies, genealogies, quotations or origin stories.
- Do not publish sensitive location or cultural information that could expose people, sites or knowledge to harm.
- Record disagreements within communities rather than inventing a single authorised voice.
- Treat repatriation, access, ownership, benefit-sharing and stewardship as substantive research questions.

## 7. Regional and community research priorities

The programme will not treat “Africa” as a single research unit. Each case study should begin with a people, place, period, language, question or source.

Initial priorities:

1. **Turkana and Ateker:** community naming, oral history, language, pastoral knowledge, land, memory, aesthetics and contemporary self-determination. Community verification is required for linguistic and cultural claims.
2. **Nile Valley and Northeast Africa:** ancient Egyptian, Nubian/Kushite and neighbouring histories, studied through their own records and regional relations.
3. **Sahara and Sahel:** mobility, pastoralism, trade, scholarship, manuscript traditions, environmental change and political formations.
4. **West and Central Africa:** language histories, states and non-state institutions, knowledge networks, art, metallurgy and diaspora histories.
5. **East Africa and the Horn:** diverse societies, coast and inland networks, pastoral and agricultural histories, maritime exchange, scholarship and political organisation.
6. **Southern Africa:** long-term population histories, language and cultural diversity, art, pastoralism, political formations and colonial encounters.
7. **Black diasporas:** self-naming, forced displacement, cultural survival, political identity, aesthetics, enterprise and transnational networks.

These are research pathways, not exhaustive classifications of peoples or cultures.

## 10. Core bibliography and research entry points

The following sources are starting points. Their inclusion does not mean every underlying argument has been fully reviewed or accepted.

### Naming, geography and identity

- South African History Online, [“Africa: What’s in a name?”](https://sahistory.org.za/article/africa-whats-name) (2015; updated 2025). Accessible overview of the history and changing geographical use of the term.
- David Stone, [“Roman Africa”](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0327), Oxford Bibliographies in Classics (2018). Scholarly reference on Roman North Africa and the complexity of Roman rule.
- Atoma Batoma, [“African Ethnonyms and Toponyms: An Annotated Bibliography”](https://doi.org/10.17077/1092-9576.1000), Electronic Journal of Africana Bibliography 10(1) (2006). Names as historical, geographical, linguistic, symbolic and socio-political records.
- John Parker and Richard Rathbone, [African History: A Very Short Introduction](https://ccaf.africa/books/%5BJohn_Parker_Richard_Rathbone%5D_African_History.pdf). Context for the history of continental naming and African historiography.
- UNESCO, [General History of Africa](https://www.unesco.org/en/general-history-africa). Multi-volume historical resource; use individual chapters and their evidence critically.

### Philosophy and intellectual traditions

- Anke Graness, [“Writing the history of philosophy in Africa: where to begin?”](https://doi.org/10.1080/13696815.2015.1053799), Journal of African Cultural Studies 28(2) (2016). Discusses methodology, ancient Egyptian texts and the historiography of African philosophy.
- Teodros Kiros, “Zera Yacob and Traditional Ethiopian Philosophy,” in Kwasi Wiredu (ed.), A Companion to African Philosophy (2004).
- Hamadou Adama, [“Ahmed Bâba at-Timbuktî”](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780190277734.013.957), Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History (2021).
- Peter Adamson and Chike Jeffers, [“From Here to Timbuktu: Sub-Saharan Islamic Philosophy”](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198927174.003.0010) (2025).
- Henry Odera Oruka, Sage Philosophy: Indigenous Thinkers and Modern Debate (1990). A major methodological reference for the documentation and analysis of philosophical sages.

### Black Is Beautiful and cultural politics

- National Museum of African American History and Culture, [“Black is Beautiful: The Emergence of Black Culture and Identity in the 60s and 70s”](https://nmaahc.si.edu/explore/stories/black-beautiful-emergence-black-culture-and-identity-60s-and-70s). Museum overview of cultural affirmation, natural hairstyles, Black arts and identity.
- Duke University Libraries, [“The Afro”](https://exhibits.library.duke.edu/exhibits/show/-black-is-beautiful-/the-afro). Archival exhibition on natural hair, representation and commercial response.
- Maxine Leeds Craig, [Ain’t I a Beauty Queen? Black Women, Beauty, and the Politics of Race](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195152623.001.0001) (2002). Black women's beauty politics, natural hair and social movements.
- Susannah Walker, “Black Is Profitable: The Commodification of the Afro, 1960–1975,” Enterprise & Society 1(3) (2000), 536–564.
- Kwame Brathwaite Archive and the African Jazz-Art Society & Studios (AJASS): primary visual and organisational materials to be inventoried with provenance and rights.

### Names, borders and classification

- [“To count or not to count? Insights from Kenya for global debates about enumerating ethnicity in national censuses”](https://doi.org/10.1177/14687968211056379). Research on ethnicity, census categories, colonial boundary-making and postcolonial enumeration in Kenya.
- Lehasa Moloi, [“Decolonising coloniality embedded theories underlying the origins of the term ‘Africa’ as a designation for the continent: An Afrocentric perspective”](https://doi.org/10.2989/NA.2026.40.1.8.1016), Nomina Africana (2026). A recent Afrocentric critique of dominant etymological narratives; treat its argument as a scholarly intervention to be compared with other linguistic and historical evidence, not as automatic consensus.

## 11. Open questions and cautions

- The exact etymology of Africa remains disputed; do not manufacture certainty.
- No single universally attested indigenous name for the entire continent has been established by the cited evidence.
- The term Black changes meaning across time, place and political context.
- Ancient populations cannot be mapped directly onto modern racial categories.
- Ethnonyms do not necessarily correspond one-to-one with languages, genetic populations or political communities.
- “Civilisation” must not be restricted to monumental architecture, centralised states or written archives.
- Oral knowledge must be documented without treating it as either infallible or inherently inferior to written sources.
- The phrase “Black Is Beautiful” has a complex movement history; do not assign a sole inventor without carefully attributed evidence.
- Commercial visibility is not equivalent to community ownership or economic sovereignty.
- Cultural affirmation must not become a new hierarchy that polices Black appearance or identity.

## 12. Working thesis

The histories of the peoples now grouped under the name Africa do not begin with that name, and they cannot be reduced to the institutions that later mapped, classified or governed them. Their lands held many societies, languages, political orders, knowledge systems, creative practices and sages. These histories are connected, contested and changing; they form a mosaic, not a monolith.

“Black = Beautiful” gives this documentation programme a contemporary ethical and cultural proposition: affirm dignity, reclaim historical memory, and strengthen the capacity of people and communities to define, preserve, own and develop their futures.

> Before the classifications, there were peoples.  
> Before the borders, there were lands and relationships.  
> Across those lands, knowledge was made, tested, taught and remembered.  
> The task is not to invent a single lost civilisation, but to document the many histories with care, evidence and respect.
