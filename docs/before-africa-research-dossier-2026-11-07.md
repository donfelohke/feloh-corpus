# BEFORE AFRICA
## Naming, identity, knowledge and political life before continental standardisation

**Research programme:** FELOH Sovereign Corpus  
**Status:** RESEARCH FRAMEWORK · living dossier  
**Date:** 7 November 2026  
**Central question:** What did African peoples call themselves, how did they understand existence, how did they organise society, and what did they know before external institutions came to define the continent and its inhabitants?

> **Working proposition:** Peoples, places, languages, institutions and knowledge traditions existed before any single continental name became dominant. The history of the name “Africa” is one history; the histories of the peoples later gathered under that name are many histories.

## 1. Purpose and scope

This programme investigates African histories from African-centred perspectives without assuming that “Africa” was a single pre-existing political, linguistic, cultural or philosophical unit. It asks how identities were named and negotiated at local, regional, transregional and diasporic scales; how people understood being, personhood, nature, community and ancestry; how authority and collective decisions were organised; and how knowledge was generated, tested, transmitted and adapted.

“Before Africa” is a research title, not a claim that the continent had no names, that its peoples lacked continental awareness, or that all relevant histories ended when the word Africa became widespread. It refers to histories that cannot be reduced to the later dominance of one external geographical label.

## 2. Naming: what can and cannot be said

The exact etymology of “Africa” is disputed. Roman usage is historically attested for a region associated with Carthage and Roman North Africa; the geographical scope later broadened over centuries. Greek writers used “Libya” for extensive parts of northern Africa, while other names—including Aethiopia, Egypt, Nubia, and later Arabic geographical terms—were applied to particular places and populations in different periods. These labels were neither interchangeable nor universal indigenous self-names.

Do not state as settled fact that:
- “Africa” has one conclusively proven Latin etymology;
- all Africans once called the continent “Alkebulan”;
- a single indigenous name covered the entire continent;
- Roman naming alone explains every later stage of continental cartography.

Treat “Afri,” “Afer,” “Ifri,” “Ifriqiya,” Latin *Africa*, Greek *Libya*, and later cartographic usage as distinct terms requiring contextual histories. “Alkebulan” appears in modern popular discourse as an alleged ancient name, but claims that it was the continent’s universal, oldest indigenous name require primary-source evidence and should not be repeated as established fact.

The question is not simply “What was the real name?” It is: who used each name, in which language, for which territory or people, at what date, in what source, and under what political conditions?

## 3. Research domains

### A. Self-naming and identity
Study endonyms, exonyms, ethnonyms, clan and lineage names, place names, language names, titles, political identities, and changing affiliations. Distinguish what people called themselves from what neighbours, merchants, chroniclers, states or colonial administrations called them. Track how names change through migration, trade, intermarriage, religious conversion, conquest, federation and state formation.

### B. Existence, personhood and moral worlds
Investigate philosophies and concepts through their own languages and historical contexts: personhood, vitality, ancestry, obligation, land, spirit, nature, time, death, justice, harmony and the good life. Examples may include ancient Egyptian discussions of *ma’at*, and southern African traditions discussed under *ubuntu/botho/hunhu*, but neither is a universal African philosophy. Do not collapse distinct traditions into one “African worldview.” “Livity” is particularly associated with Rastafari and should be treated as a modern, historically situated term when used as a comparative lens—not projected unchanged onto all ancient societies.

### C. Governance and social organisation
Document the diversity of political forms: kingdoms, empires, city-states, confederations, councils, lineage-based systems, age-grade institutions, chiefdoms, pastoral governance, trading polities, village assemblies and decentralised or stateless formations. Avoid treating centralised statehood as the only measure of political complexity. Ask how authority was legitimised, checked, inherited, elected, negotiated or resisted; how law, land, labour, taxation, conflict resolution and public goods were managed.

### D. Knowledge systems and technologies
Investigate agriculture, pastoralism, ecology, medicine, pharmacology, metallurgy, ceramics, architecture, mathematics, astronomy, navigation, hydraulics, textiles, trade, food preservation, oral literature, music, dance, law and pedagogy. Record the methods by which knowledge was observed, tested, transmitted, specialised, safeguarded and revised. Do not romanticise tradition or assume all inherited practices were effective, egalitarian or static.

### E. Memory, evidence and historiography
Use archaeology, archaeobotany, archaeometallurgy, linguistics, inscriptions, manuscripts, oral traditions, material culture, environmental history, genetics where appropriate, and critical reading of external accounts. No single evidence type is sufficient. Oral traditions are historical evidence that require contextual analysis, not either automatic fact or disposable myth. Written sources are not automatically neutral; examine authorship, audience, genre, translation, patronage and power.

## 4. Regional research map

This is a comparative map, not an exhaustive inventory:
- Nile Valley: Kemet / ancient Egypt, Nubia, Kush, Meroë and neighbouring societies.
- North Africa: Amazigh-speaking societies, Carthage, Roman provincial histories and trans-Saharan networks.
- Northeast and Horn: Aksum, Red Sea networks, highland and lowland societies.
- West Africa: Ghana/Wagadu, Mali, Songhai, Hausa city-states, Yoruba, Akan and many others.
- Central Africa: Kongo, Luba, Kuba and diverse forest, riverine and savanna societies.
- East Africa: Swahili city-states, Great Lakes polities, pastoral societies, coastal and inland trade networks, and communities of the Horn and Rift Valley.
- Southern Africa: Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe, Khoekhoe, San and Bantu-speaking societies, among others.
- Islands and Indian Ocean: Madagascar and western Indian Ocean networks.
- Diasporas: histories formed through forced displacement, migration, cultural survival and new political identities; do not erase the distinct history of continental societies.

For East African and Turkana-related research, identify specific communities, language varieties, historical periods and sources before making claims. Do not invent or generalise indigenous words.

## 5. Conceptual frame: ROME / BLACK / LIVITY

These are interpretive lenses for this project, not neutral historical categories or exhaustive descriptions of whole populations.

- **ROME — institutionalisation:** a shorthand for examining imperial administration, territorial classification, legal ordering, taxation, military power and the institutional production of legibility. Roman North Africa is a case study, not the sole origin of African statecraft or institutions.
- **BLACK — livity:** a philosophical and political proposition about living agency, embodied knowledge, ancestral continuity, creativity and self-determination. “Black” has multiple historical meanings and should not be treated as a timeless, uniform identity.
- **UNNAMED — not reducible to an imposed label:** the fact that peoples had their own names and identities before a dominant external continental designation.
- **UNTAMED — agency:** the capacity to adapt, refuse, negotiate, govern, create and define collective life; not a claim that African societies were isolated from exchange or free of hierarchy.

The research should test these propositions against evidence. It should not force evidence to fit them.

## 6. Method and evidence protocol

For every claim, record:
1. Exact claim and scope.
2. People, place, language and period.
3. Primary source or material evidence, where available.
4. Scholarly interpretation and competing explanations.
5. Source limitations, translation issues and provenance.
6. Confidence label: VERIFIED, CORROBORATED, DISPUTED, UNVERIFIED, INTERPRETATION, OPINION or TESTIMONY.
7. Maturity label: RESEARCH, HISTORICAL, PROPOSAL or UNKNOWN.

Use “not yet established” where evidence is insufficient. Separate a community’s own account, a later oral tradition, an outsider’s description and a modern scholarly reconstruction. Do not convert a hypothesis into a fact through repetition.

## 7. Questions to investigate

- What names did people use for themselves, their neighbours, territories and political communities?
- Which names survive in local languages, inscriptions, manuscripts and oral histories?
- How did trade, migration, religion, empire and colonial administration alter naming?
- What concepts of personhood, vitality, ancestry, land and moral obligation are attested in particular traditions?
- How were authority, consensus, accountability, property, justice and conflict resolution organised?
- What knowledge was held by farmers, herders, healers, smiths, builders, navigators, traders, artists and specialist lineages?
- How were knowledge and skills transmitted, tested, revised and protected?
- Which institutions were locally developed, borrowed, adapted or imposed?
- How have African scholars and communities challenged inherited historiographies?

## 9. Research commitments

1. Plurality over a single civilisational story.
2. African agency without romanticisation.
3. Indigenous terminology with language, place and date attached.
4. Evidence before etymological certainty.
5. Distinguish historical description from contemporary political interpretation.
6. No invented quotations, translations, universal names or cultural claims.
7. Credit African scholars, knowledge holders, communities and source custodians.
8. Respect restrictions on sacred, private, communal or culturally sensitive knowledge.
9. Record uncertainty and disagreement visibly.
10. Keep the research open to revision.

## 10. Working thesis

The histories of African peoples do not begin with the word “Africa,” nor are they exhausted by the institutions that later named, mapped, classified or governed them. The task is to recover, compare and critically understand the many ways people named themselves, understood existence, organised collective life and developed knowledge—while recognising exchange, conflict, change and internal diversity.

**UNNAMED · UNTAMED · ALIVE** is the project's interpretive refrain. It is not a substitute for evidence.
