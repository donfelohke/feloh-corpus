# BEFORE AFRICA
## Naming, identity, knowledge, populations and political life before continental standardisation

**Research programme:** FELOH Sovereign Corpus  
**Status:** RESEARCH FRAMEWORK · living dossier  
**Updated:** 11 November 2026  
**Central question:** What did African peoples call themselves, how did they understand existence, how did they organise society, what populations and knowledge systems existed, and what did they know before external institutions came to define the continent and its inhabitants?

> **Working proposition:** Peoples, places, languages, institutions and knowledge traditions existed before any single continental name became dominant. The history of the name “Africa” is one history; the histories of the peoples later gathered under that name are many histories.

## 1. Purpose and scope

This programme investigates African histories from African-centred perspectives without assuming that “Africa” was a single pre-existing political, linguistic, cultural or philosophical unit. It asks how identities were named and negotiated; how people understood being, personhood, nature, community and ancestry; how authority and collective decisions were organised; how knowledge was generated, tested, transmitted and adapted; and what the archaeological, genetic and linguistic records show about population diversity and continuity.

“Before Africa” is a research title, not a claim that the continent had no names, that its peoples lacked continental awareness, or that all relevant histories ended when the word Africa became widespread. It refers to histories that cannot be reduced to the later dominance of one external geographical label.

**Key distinction maintained throughout:** the antiquity of African peoples and heritage is real; the modern category “Black” is not an unchanged biological identity extending intact into prehistory. Deep African ancestry, dark pigmentation, population diversity and cultural continuity can be investigated without forcing ancient people into modern racial categories.

## 2. Naming: what can and cannot be said

The exact etymology of “Africa” is disputed. Roman usage is historically attested for a region associated with Carthage and Roman North Africa after 146 BCE; the geographical scope later broadened over centuries. Greek writers used “Libya” for extensive parts of northern Africa, while other names—including Aethiopia, Egypt/Kemet, Nubia/Kush, Punt, and later Arabic geographical terms—were applied to particular places and populations in different periods. These labels were neither interchangeable nor universal indigenous self-names.

Do not state as settled fact that:
- “Africa” has one conclusively proven Latin etymology;
- all Africans once called the continent “Alkebulan”;
- a single indigenous name covered the entire continent;
- Roman naming alone explains every later stage of continental cartography.

Treat “Afri,” “Afer,” “Ifri,” “Ifriqiya,” Latin *Africa*, Greek *Libya*, and later cartographic usage as distinct terms requiring contextual histories. “Alkebulan” appears in modern popular discourse as an alleged ancient name, but claims that it was the continent’s universal, oldest indigenous name require primary-source evidence and should not be repeated as established fact.

The question is not simply “What was the real name?” It is: who used each name, in which language, for which territory or people, at what date, in what source, and under what political conditions?

### Pre-146 BCE attested regional names (summary)

| Name | Context | What it establishes |
|------|---------|---------------------|
| Kemet | Ancient Egyptian | Nile Valley land (“Black Land”); not the continent |
| Kush | Egyptian & Kushite sources | Middle Nile kingdom/region; Napatan royal inscriptions provide Indigenous political memory |
| Punt | Egyptian records | Red Sea / Horn region of exchange; boundaries debated |
| Tjehenu / Tjehemu, Libu, Meshwesh | Egyptian texts | Western neighbours; Libu probable source of Greek “Libya” |
| Afri | Associated with Carthaginian hinterland | Influential (not settled) etymology for later “Africa” |
| Libya (Greek) | Classical geography | North African lands west of Egypt; extent varied |
| Aithiopes / Ethiopia | Greek | Peoples/lands to the south; scope variable |

No single documented Indigenous continental name is established by the primary record before 146 BCE.

## 3. Continental timeline and regional histories

Africa did not have one prehistoric history. It had many histories unfolding simultaneously across different environments, with connections, exchanges, migrations and independent cultural developments.

### Approximate chronology

- **Late Pleistocene (50,000–12,000 BP):** Deep human histories; regional stone-tool traditions, symbolic practices, exchange networks.
- **Early Holocene (12,000–8,000 BP):** African Humid Period begins; Sahara increasingly humid; lakes and grasslands expand; pottery and aquatic-resource economies.
- **Middle Holocene (8,000–5,000 BP):** Green Sahara at height and transition; fishing, hunting, gathering and pastoralism coexist; livestock herding spreads through networks and local adaptations.
- **Later Holocene (after ~5,000 BP):** Aridification reshapes Saharan settlement; distinct histories develop along the Nile, Maghreb, Sahel, West Africa and eastern Africa.

Chronology is regional rather than universal.

### Regional framework (not modern borders)

1. **North Africa and the Maghreb** — Iberomaurusian and Capsian traditions; Taforalt; deep indigenous history before Phoenician, Greek or Roman presence.
2. **Sahara and Sahel** — Green Sahara aquatic and pastoral economies; Takarkori, Gobero; rock art; cultural diffusion of pastoralism into established populations.
3. **Nile Valley and Nubia** — Early Khartoum, Nabta Playa, Kushite sequences; Egyptian and Nubian histories studied in their own right and in relation to African neighbours.
4. **West Africa** — Early Holocene pottery (Ounjougou, Mali, before 9400 BCE); watercraft; later farming and complex settlements.
5. **Central Africa** — Forest-adapted foraging; long but unevenly documented sequences; Congo Basin as historically dynamic, not a blank space.
6. **Eastern Africa and the Horn** — Lithic exchange; Pastoral Neolithic diversity (Elmenteitan, Savanna Pastoral Neolithic); Lake Turkana pastoral arrival ~5,000 BP.
7. **Southern Africa** — Continuous Later Stone Age hunter-gatherer histories; rock art; later interaction with herders and farmers.

Pottery, pastoralism and agriculture did not arrive as one package across the continent.

## 4. Populations, ancestry and physical anthropology

### Ancient DNA highlights

- **Takarkori (2025, Nature):** Two ~7,000-year-old Pastoral Neolithic women from southwestern Libya. Majority ancestry from a previously unknown, deeply divergent North African lineage that diverged from sub-Saharan lineages around the time of Out-of-Africa dispersals and remained largely isolated. Close affinity to 15,000-year-old Taforalt (Iberomaurusian) foragers. Limited evidence of substantial sub-Saharan gene flow during the African Humid Period in these samples. Pastoralism interpreted as cultural diffusion into an established North African population. Low but detectable Neanderthal ancestry.
- **Southern African genomes (2026, Nature):** 28 ancient genomes (~10,200–150 BP) show substantial genetic continuity over at least 9,000 years in sampled populations, with deep differentiation from other African groups. Some ancient variants not equivalently represented in present-day populations.
- **Sub-Saharan forager structure:** Stable three-way structure (eastern, southern, and a component strongest in central African rainforest hunter-gatherers) over ~18,000 years with limited long-range gene flow once established.

Cultural change frequently occurred through diffusion and local adoption rather than wholesale population replacement.

### Physical anthropology

Regional morphological diversity is well attested. Southern African Later Stone Age and descendant Khoe-San groups show small adult body size continuous from the terminal Pleistocene. Late Pleistocene specimens (Hofmeyr, Iwo Eleru) display features placing them outside simple modern regional clusters. Morphometric studies caution against equating archaeological cultures with fixed racial types.

## 5. Research domains

### A. Self-naming and identity
Endonyms, exonyms, ethnonyms, clan and lineage names, place names, language names, titles, political identities, and changing affiliations. Distinguish self-names from neighbour, merchant, chronicler, state or colonial labels.

### B. Existence, personhood and moral worlds
Philosophies and concepts in their own languages and contexts: personhood, vitality, ancestry, obligation, land, spirit, nature, time, death, justice. Examples (scoped, not universal): ancient Egyptian *ma’at*; southern African ubuntu/botho/hunhu debates. “Livity” is a modern Rastafari-associated term usable as a comparative lens, not a pan-African ancient word.

### C. Governance and social organisation
Kingdoms, empires, city-states, confederations, councils, lineage systems, age-grade institutions, pastoral governance, trading polities, village assemblies, decentralised formations. Avoid treating centralised statehood as the only measure of complexity.

### D. Knowledge systems and technologies
Agriculture, pastoralism, ecology, medicine, metallurgy, ceramics, architecture, mathematics, astronomy, navigation, textiles, trade, oral literature, music, dance, law, pedagogy. Record methods of observation, testing, transmission and revision.

### E. Memory, evidence and historiography
Archaeology, archaeobotany, archaeometallurgy, linguistics, inscriptions, manuscripts, oral traditions, material culture, environmental history, genetics (where appropriate), and critical reading of external accounts. Oral traditions are historical sources requiring contextual analysis.

### F. Resilience as material achievement
Climate adaptation, diversified food systems, experimental technologies, long-distance material procurement, intergenerational knowledge transmission. Resilience is studied through concrete evidence, not slogans.

## 6. Heritage forms (must be kept distinct)

1. **Biological heritage** — ancestry, population history, adaptation.
2. **Material heritage** — tools, ceramics, settlements, burials, art, technologies.
3. **Linguistic heritage** — language families, change, borrowing, transmission.
4. **Intellectual heritage** — ecological knowledge, medicine, astronomy, engineering, philosophy.
5. **Living heritage** — oral history, music, ritual, craft, law, kinship, community memory.

These do not always move together. DNA alone cannot reconstruct African history.

## 7. Languages (major groupings — classifications remain research-active)

- **Afroasiatic** — highest diversity in Northeast Africa; branches include Egyptian, Berber, Cushitic, Chadic, Omotic, Semitic.
- **Nilo-Saharan** — geographically fragmented; coherence still debated; possible early associations with Aquatic traditions; later Nilotic expansions better documented.
- **Niger-Congo** (including Bantu) — dominant across much of West, Central and Southern Africa; Bantu expansions after ~4,000–3,000 BP thoroughly studied.
- **Khoe-Kwadi** and related southern African click-language groups — associated with long-term southern African forager and later pastoral histories.

Language shift, bilingualism and substrate influence mean archaeological cultures cannot be mapped one-to-one onto modern language families.

## 8. The modern category “Black”

Ancient African populations existed long before modern racial classifications. Skin pigmentation, ancestry, geography, language, cultural identity and political belonging are related but different subjects. European racial ideology increasingly used physical appearance and ancestry to organise human difference and justify domination, particularly through the Atlantic slave trade and colonialism.

Consequently:
- Ancient Africans did not all share one racial identity.
- Dark pigmentation has a deep evolutionary history but does not by itself identify language, culture or ancestry.
- The modern African diaspora has its own history of displacement, resistance, cultural survival and identity formation.
- Ancient African achievements do not need reassignment to a modern racial category to establish their importance.

The stronger historical argument is that the heritage now claimed and celebrated by Black communities has roots much older than the racial systems that later attempted to define, rank and control African people.

## 9. Archive, authority and urgency

African history is not preserved equally. Sites face development, looting, climate change, conflict and neglect. Oral traditions can disappear with knowledge holders. Collections and human remains may be held far from source communities. Genomic research raises questions of priority-setting, sample control, interpretation and benefit. Greater African scholarly and community leadership in ancient-DNA and heritage research is required.

The task is not merely discovery but establishing durable, accountable structures of stewardship and interpretation.

## 11. Method and evidence protocol

For every claim, record:
1. Exact claim and scope.
2. People, place, language and period.
3. Primary source or material evidence, where available.
4. Scholarly interpretation and competing explanations.
5. Source limitations, translation issues and provenance.
6. Confidence label: VERIFIED, CORROBORATED, DISPUTED, UNVERIFIED, INTERPRETATION, OPINION or TESTIMONY.
7. Maturity label: RESEARCH, HISTORICAL, PROPOSAL or UNKNOWN.

Use “not yet established” where evidence is insufficient. Separate a community’s own account, a later oral tradition, an outsider’s description and a modern scholarly reconstruction.

## 13. Research commitments

1. Plurality over a single civilisational story.
2. African agency without romanticisation.
3. Indigenous terminology with language, place and date attached.
4. Evidence before etymological certainty.
5. Distinguish historical description from contemporary political interpretation.
6. No invented quotations, translations, universal names or cultural claims.
7. Credit African scholars, knowledge holders, communities and source custodians.
8. Respect restrictions on sacred, private, communal or culturally sensitive knowledge.
9. Record uncertainty and disagreement visibly.
10. Keep the research open to revision.

## 14. Working thesis

The histories of African peoples do not begin with the word “Africa,” nor are they exhausted by the institutions that later named, mapped, classified or governed them. The task is to recover, compare and critically understand the many ways people named themselves, understood existence, organised collective life, developed knowledge, adapted to changing environments and maintained continuity—while recognising exchange, conflict, change and internal diversity.

**UNNAMED · UNTAMED · ALIVE** is the project's interpretive refrain. It is not a substitute for evidence.
