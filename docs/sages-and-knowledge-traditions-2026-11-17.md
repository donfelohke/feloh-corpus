> **Foundational proposition:** African knowledge traditions did not begin when they were written down, translated, collected by outsiders or admitted into universities. They have been created, tested, taught, debated, embodied and transmitted through many intellectual practices and institutions.

> **Research commitment:** Recover the people who think, teach, remember, interpret, make, test and transmit knowledge. Name them where they consent and where evidence permits. Preserve their words and intellectual agency. Do not turn living communities into anonymous repositories of cultural data.

## 1. Purpose and scope

This dossier establishes a research method and programme for documenting sages, philosophers, scholars, teachers, healers, historians, artists, craftspeople, navigators, pastoral experts, ecological observers, builders, legal thinkers, ritual specialists, storytellers and other knowledge custodians across African societies and diasporas.

It rejects two errors:

1. The claim that philosophy and systematic reasoning are absent from societies whose intellectual work is transmitted orally, practically, communally or in languages excluded from formal institutions.
2. The opposite error of romanticising every inherited belief or practice as timeless, uniform, infallible or beyond criticism.

Knowledge must be taken seriously enough to be documented accurately and examined critically. Respect does not require abandoning analysis; analysis does not justify extraction or contempt.

This is a research framework, not a claim to have completed an inventory of African sages. The people and traditions named below are entry points for further investigation, not a definitive canon.

## 2. What counts as knowledge?

The programme uses a broad, plural account of intellectual work. Depending on the society and research question, knowledge may be expressed through:

- spoken argument, dialogue, debate, testimony and public deliberation;
- proverbs, poetry, songs, praise, narrative, genealogy and oral history;
- manuscripts, inscriptions, archives, letters, legal records and scholarly commentary;
- observation, experiment, diagnosis, classification and prediction;
- ecological practice, animal husbandry, land and water management;
- craft, construction, metallurgy, textiles, food systems and material technologies;
- movement, music, dance, performance and embodied instruction;
- ritual, spiritual practice, ethical reasoning and interpretation;
- apprenticeship, demonstration, imitation, correction and intergenerational teaching;
- institutions that preserve, distribute, contest or regulate knowledge.

These forms are not mutually exclusive. A single knowledge tradition may use speech, material practice, memory, writing, observation and collective deliberation together.

**Working distinction:** a knowledge practice, a body of knowledge, a knowledge custodian, a sage and a philosopher are related but not interchangeable categories. The dossier records what a person does and how their ideas work before assigning a title.

## 3. Philosophic sagacity and the Kenyan foundation

### 3.1 Henry Odera Oruka and the Sage Philosophy Project

Kenyan philosopher **Henry Odera Oruka (1944–1995)** developed the Sage Philosophy Project at the University of Nairobi from the mid-1970s. The project sought to document individuals in African communities who could critically examine and reason about fundamental questions, rather than treating a community's beliefs as one anonymous collective philosophy.

Oruka's work is especially relevant to BLACK DOCS because it:

- recognises that rigorous thought can exist outside universities and written philosophical canons;
- treats sages as identifiable people with their own arguments and intellectual agency;
- uses dialogue and recorded interviews as evidence;
- makes the researcher's questions and editorial interventions visible;
- invites philosophical engagement, comparison, disagreement and critique;
- challenges the assumption that literacy is a prerequisite for reasoned thought.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on African Sage Philosophy and the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's account provide useful scholarly introductions to the project, its rationale and its debates.

### 3.2 Folk sages and philosophic sages

Oruka distinguished between forms of sagacity. A person recognised as wise because they preserve and communicate communal knowledge is not automatically the same as a person who critically examines, questions or revises the assumptions of that knowledge. The distinction is analytical, not a hierarchy of human worth.

For this dossier:

- **Knowledge custodian:** a person or community entrusted with preserving, practising or transmitting knowledge.
- **Folk sage:** a person recognised for wisdom in articulating and interpreting a community's inherited beliefs, values and practices.
- **Philosophic sage:** a person who demonstrates reflective, reasoned and critical examination of beliefs, concepts, values or institutions.
- **Professional philosopher or scholar:** a person working through formal or organised intellectual institutions; this status does not make their work inherently superior to community-based thought.
- **Practitioner-expert:** a person with demonstrable expertise developed through sustained practice, observation, apprenticeship and correction.

These categories can overlap. They should never be assigned solely by age, status, title, gender, occupation or a researcher's expectation.

### 3.3 Methodological debates

Oruka's project is influential, not beyond critique. Research must engage questions such as:

- Does an interview capture a thinker's own concepts, or partly produce them through the interviewer's questions and translation?
- How should an individual sage's views be related to community traditions without treating either as identical?
- What counts as philosophical argument in a particular language?
- How can a researcher distinguish critical reflection from recitation without imposing an external examination format?
- What is lost when dialogue is excerpted, translated, transcribed or published?
- How should gender, class, age, authority and access shape whose knowledge becomes visible?
- How can recorded material remain open to criticism while respecting the thinker and community?

The project should preserve the speaker's own reasoning, not manufacture a local Socrates or force every intellectual tradition into a European philosophical template.

## 4. Intellectual traditions are plural

The phrase **African philosophy** names a field of debate, not a single doctrine shared by every African society. The same applies to African science, African law, African spirituality, African aesthetics and African knowledge.

Research must distinguish at least the following:

1. **Indigenous and community-based intellectual traditions** — situated knowledge developed and transmitted within particular societies.
2. **Written scholarly traditions** — manuscripts, libraries, schools, commentaries, jurisprudence, theology, philosophy and scientific writing.
3. **Court, state and institutional knowledge** — political thought, diplomacy, administration, law, record-keeping and public deliberation.
4. **Practical and technical knowledge** — ecology, agriculture, pastoralism, medicine, engineering, craft and navigation.
5. **Religious and ethical traditions** — local, regional and transregional systems of interpretation and debate.
6. **Modern African philosophical movements** — professional philosophy, sage philosophy, nationalist-ideological philosophy, critical theory and other approaches.
7. **Diasporic and transnational traditions** — knowledge shaped through migration, enslavement, resistance, exchange and new cultural formations.

The boundaries between these categories are porous. The purpose is to make the source and context of each claim visible, not to create rigid compartments.

## 5. Research pathways and regional case studies

### 5.1 Turkana and Ateker: a priority research pathway

Turkana and wider Ateker histories are a priority for this corpus. This is a programme of research, not a claim that one person or institution can speak for all Turkana or Ateker communities.

Potential fields of investigation include:

- oral histories of people, place, migration, settlement, kinship and historical change;
- pastoral knowledge, livestock care, herd management and mobility;
- reading seasonal, ecological and environmental change;
- water, pasture, routes, landscape memory and risk;
- wild and cultivated foods, plant knowledge and food security;
- craft, material culture, adornment, aesthetics and technical skill;
- language, naming, classification, narrative and proverbial reasoning;
- education, apprenticeship, intergenerational learning and contemporary knowledge change;
- women's knowledge, labour, authority and transmission;
- political leadership, deliberation, conflict resolution and historical memory;
- encounters with colonial administration, missions, schooling, development programmes and the modern state.

Published work gives several useful starting points:

- John Teria Ng'asike's research documents Turkana children's sociocultural practices, elders' knowledge and the relationship between pastoral life and formal science education.
- John Lamphear's work on Turkana origin and expansion shows how oral traditions can preserve complex histories of migration and social formation, while also cautioning against a single, monolithic origin narrative.
- Research on Turkana pastoralists' environmental knowledge documents observation, memory, mobility, risk management and intergenerational learning.
- Research on Turkana ecological uncertainty and divination examines how people interpret changing conditions and make decisions under uncertainty.
- Studies of Turkana wild edible plants document detailed plant knowledge and its relation to food security.
- Mads Yding's research on Turkana chiefs in the colonial contact zone combines archival materials with oral history to examine local leadership under colonial occupation.

These sources are not substitutes for community-held knowledge or direct consent-based research. They are leads to be critically reviewed, compared and extended with community participation.

**Important distinction:** Turkana is not a synonym for Ateker as a whole. The wider Ateker field includes distinct communities and histories. Similarities, language relationships and shared traditions must be demonstrated in each case, not presumed.

### 5.2 Wider regional research

The following are research pathways rather than exhaustive lists:

| Region / historical setting | Questions and source traditions |
|---|---|
| Nile Valley and Northeast Africa | Instruction texts, inscriptions, religious and political thought, Nubian and Egyptian histories, neighbouring exchanges |
| Horn of Africa | Ge'ez manuscript traditions, Ethiopian philosophy and theology, legal and historical writing |
| Sahel and West Africa | Timbuktu and other scholarly networks, oral historians, jurists, statecraft, Arabic and African-language manuscripts |
| Akan and neighbouring societies | Conceptual analysis in African languages, ethics, political thought and oral traditions |
| Yoruba and neighbouring societies | Ifa intellectual traditions, poetry, divination, ethics, history and performance |
| Central Africa | Political thought, oral histories, ecological knowledge, craft and material technologies |
| East African coast | Swahili scholarship, maritime knowledge, law, poetry, trade and multilingual exchange |
| Great Lakes | Oral historiography, court traditions, political institutions, agriculture and medicine |
| Southern Africa | Philosophical reflection, oral literature, ecological and technical knowledge, political and legal thought |
| African diasporas | Intellectual traditions formed through displacement, resistance, adaptation, cultural continuity and new institutions |

A region is not a people, and a people is not a single school of thought. Each case study requires a defined community, place, language, period and question.

## 6. Sages, scholars and intellectual biographies

The following people are useful initial case studies. Their inclusion does not imply that they belong to one school or that they exhaust the traditions in question.

### 6.1 Henry Odera Oruka (Kenya, 1944–1995)

**Research focus:** Sage Philosophy, philosophic sagacity, oral dialogue, African philosophy and the epistemic status of community-based thinkers.

**Why included:** His project provides a documented Kenyan methodology for recording individual sages and critically engaging their arguments.

**Questions:** How did the project select interlocutors? How were their words translated and edited? What did the sages themselves say about the interview process? How did the project handle gender and regional representation?

### 6.2 Zera Yacob (Ethiopia, 1599–1692)

**Research focus:** The Hatata, reason, religion, ethics, inquiry and the intellectual setting of seventeenth-century Ethiopia.

**Why included:** His work is an important case for the history of written philosophical reflection in an African language and manuscript environment.

**Questions:** What is known about the manuscript transmission and authorship? How should the work be situated in Ethiopian, African, Christian, Islamic and global intellectual histories without reducing it to a simple European comparison?

### 6.3 Ahmed Baba (Timbuktu, 1556–1627)

**Research focus:** Scholarship, jurisprudence, theology, intellectual networks, libraries, political power and the consequences of conquest.

**Why included:** His life and works illuminate scholarly institutions and manuscript cultures in the Sahel.

**Questions:** Which texts survive? What were their audiences and circulation networks? How did political displacement affect scholarly life and collections?

### 6.4 Ptahhotep and ancient Egyptian instruction traditions

**Research focus:** Ethical instruction, social relations, speech, authority, learning and textual transmission.

**Why included:** The instruction literature associated with Ptahhotep is an important ancient textual tradition. Its dating, textual history and attribution require specialist treatment.

**Questions:** What can be established from surviving manuscripts? How did scribal transmission shape the text? How should ancient Egyptian intellectual history be situated in its own linguistic and political context?

### 6.5 Augustine of Hippo (354–430 CE)

**Research focus:** Philosophy, theology, memory, ethics, political thought and late antique North African intellectual networks.

**Why included:** Augustine belongs to the history of Roman North Africa and its multilingual, religious and political world. His identity should not be reduced to modern racial categories.

**Questions:** What were the North African intellectual institutions and social settings of his time? How did local, Latin, Greek and Christian traditions interact?

### 6.6 Additional research leads

- **Socrates of African philosophical comparison:** not as a model to which African thinkers must conform, but as a prompt to examine how intellectual canons are constructed.
- **Kwasi Wiredu:** conceptual decolonisation, language, logic, ethics and comparative philosophy.
- **Paulin Hountondji:** critique of ethnophilosophy, scientific knowledge production and intellectual autonomy.
- **Sophie Oluwole:** Yoruba philosophy, oral intellectual traditions and the history of philosophical comparison.
- **The Grandmother's Knowledge project and other community-led archives:** investigate only with verifiable institutional and community sources; do not assume a project title or attribution without evidence.
- **Local and unnamed knowledge custodians:** research subjects whose names and identities may be documented only with consent, and whose knowledge should not be made public merely because it was recorded.

## 7. Knowledge transmission: institutions, practice and memory

Knowledge does not survive through ideas alone. It requires social and material arrangements.

Document, where relevant:

- family and intergenerational teaching;
- apprenticeship and specialist training;
- age-set, guild, professional and community institutions;
- councils, courts, assemblies and deliberative spaces;
- schools, universities, libraries, manuscript collections and archives;
- ritual, performance, song, dance and public memory;
- landscape, routes, landmarks, buildings and material objects;
- trade, migration, correspondence and scholarly networks;
- contemporary community organisations and digital archives.

Do not assume that all knowledge is freely accessible. Some knowledge is restricted by initiation, professional responsibility, sacred status, gender, family or community protocols. A responsible archive records the existence and custodianship of restricted knowledge without disclosing its contents.

## 8. Research protocol: documenting a sage or knowledge tradition

Each profile or case study should follow this protocol.

### 8.1 Subject and context

- Preferred name and spelling, including the person's own form where known.
- Pronunciation and language, where the person or community permits.
- Community, place and historical period, with uncertainty stated.
- Self-description and preferred attribution.
- Role, practice, training and relevant institutional context.
- Whether the record concerns a living person, deceased person, collective or historical figure.

### 8.2 Knowledge and argument

- What knowledge or practice is being documented?
- What question or problem does it address?
- What evidence, observation, reasoning or experience supports it?
- How is the knowledge tested, revised, taught or challenged?
- What are the relevant concepts in the original language?
- What distinctions does the knowledge holder make?
- What disagreements or alternative interpretations exist?

### 8.3 Recording and editorial method

- Who asked the questions and who answered?
- What was the interview or observation setting?
- What languages were used?
- Who translated, transcribed and edited?
- Are recordings, notes or transcripts available, and under what permissions?
- What has been omitted, summarised or anonymised?
- Has the knowledge holder or community had an opportunity to review the record?
- Are there restrictions on quotation, circulation, location or reuse?

### 8.4 Evidence and status

Use the corpus's existing labels: VERIFIED, CORROBORATED, DISPUTED, UNVERIFIED, INTERPRETATION, OPINION, TESTIMONY and PROPOSAL.

Distinguish a person's testimony about an event from independent confirmation of the event. Distinguish a community's account of its own history from an outsider's classification of that account. Preserve disagreements and uncertainty rather than forcing consensus.

## 9. Ethics, consent, ownership and benefit

The programme adopts a community-centred approach.

1. **Prior informed consent:** explain the purpose, audience, recording method, risks, intended publication and possible future reuse before collecting material.
2. **Ongoing consent:** allow participants to revise permissions, restrict circulation or withdraw material where feasible and consistent with agreed protocols.
3. **Attribution:** use the person's and community's preferred names and forms of credit. Anonymity is an option, not a default imposed for researcher convenience.
4. **Collective knowledge:** individual consent may not be sufficient for knowledge held collectively or subject to community protocols.
5. **Sensitive material:** do not publish sacred, restricted, private, medicinal, ecological or geographically sensitive information without appropriate permission and risk assessment.
6. **Language:** preserve original-language records where possible. Translation must be attributed and treated as interpretation.
7. **Ownership and rights:** clarify copyright, database rights, custodianship, access, licensing and future reuse before publication.
8. **Benefit and reciprocity:** consider community copies, accessible outputs, local capacity, fair compensation, co-authorship and other benefits agreed with participants.
9. **Correction and contestation:** provide a process for people to challenge errors, context, attribution and representation.
10. **No extraction:** do not convert community knowledge into a public dataset, commercial asset or model-training corpus merely because it has been observed or recorded.

This dossier is a research framework, not an ethics approval. Fieldwork requires appropriate institutional and community permissions.

## 11. Initial research questions and deliverables

### Questions

1. How can a corpus document a thinker's reasoning without turning the person into an ethnographic specimen?
2. How should oral intellectual traditions be transcribed, translated, cited and preserved?
3. What are the distinct roles of elders, specialists, teachers, women knowledge holders, practitioners and recognised sages in a given community?
4. How can the corpus document disagreement and intellectual change within a community?
5. What forms of knowledge are held in landscapes, livelihoods, material practices and embodied instruction?
6. How did colonial institutions change the authority, circulation and recognition of particular knowledge traditions?
7. How can local-language archives and community-controlled digital tools support continuity without exposing restricted knowledge?
8. What would a consent-based, community-governed Turkana/Ateker knowledge archive require?

### Planned outputs

- **Sage Profile Register:** individually attributed biographies and intellectual profiles.
- **Dialogue and Oral History Protocol:** consent, language, recording, transcription, translation and review.
- **Turkana/Ateker Knowledge Research Map:** research questions, existing sources, community partners and gaps; not a public map of sensitive knowledge sites.
- **Knowledge Traditions Atlas:** regional and thematic case studies, with provenance and uncertainty.
- **Manuscript and Text Register:** works, editions, languages, collections, translations and access rights.
- **Concepts and Language Register:** original terms, contextual meanings, usage and translation debates.
- **Institutions and Transmission Register:** schools, councils, families, guilds, archives and other knowledge institutions.
- **Community Archive Governance Model:** custodianship, consent, access, rights, correction and benefit-sharing.

## 12. Working thesis

African intellectual history is not confined to the written works that survived in institutions recognised by later universities. It includes people who reasoned, questioned, observed, interpreted, taught, built, healed, navigated, composed, governed and transmitted knowledge in many languages and forms.

The task is neither to invent a single African philosophy nor to romanticise an undifferentiated past. It is to document distinct thinkers and traditions, recover their contexts, preserve their own concepts and arguments, and enable critical engagement without dispossession.

The Kenyan Sage Philosophy Project demonstrates the importance of recording individual thinkers and their arguments. Turkana research demonstrates the intellectual depth of situated environmental knowledge, oral history, pastoral expertise and intergenerational learning. These are starting points for a much wider, plural and community-governed programme.

> A sage is not made a thinker by an archive.  
> A tradition is not made intelligent by translation.  
> A people do not need external permission to possess knowledge.  
> Documentation should make knowledge more visible without taking it away from its custodians.
