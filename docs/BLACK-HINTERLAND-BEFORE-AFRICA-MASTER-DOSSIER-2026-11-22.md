# BLACK HINTERLAND — BEFORE AFRICA
## Deep time, ancestral geography, African peoples, contemporary realities and sovereign futures

**Programme:** BEFORE AFRICA · BLACKS: THE DOCS & DATA  
**Canonical owner:** FELOH Sovereign Corpus  
**Author/editor:** Felix Lwova · Don Feloh  
**Created:** 22 November 2026  
**Status:** RESEARCH FRAMEWORK / SYNTHESIS — not a completed continental history, field study, census or archaeological survey  
**Scope:** African continent and Global Africa; deep prehistory to present, with future scenarios to 2100.

> **Working proposition:** Before the historical geographical name Africa acquired continental scope, the land already held evolving ecosystems, hominin populations, Homo sapiens communities, languages, technologies, territories, knowledge traditions and political formations. The name did not create the land or its peoples. The task is to investigate these histories through evidence, without projecting later racial, national or colonial categories backwards.

## 1. Purpose and title discipline

**Black Hinterland** is the programme's interpretive title for deep African population histories, ancestral landscapes and societies whose histories are often marginalised by coastal, imperial, colonial or nation-state narratives. “Hinterland” does not mean empty, backward, peripheral or dependent on a coast. Interior regions had their own centres, institutions, networks and historical agency.

**Before Africa** is a chronological and historiographical lens, not a claim that the continent did not exist before the name, that African peoples lacked regional or wider identities, or that a single universal indigenous continental name has been lost.

**Black** is used as a present-day research and political-cultural frame connecting African histories and the wider Black world. Modern racial categories are historically formed and must not be imposed unchanged on prehistoric populations. Investigate pigmentation, ancestry, population structure, self-identification, racialisation and diaspora histories as related but distinct questions.

The programme must not collapse:
- continent, region, territory, polity, people, language family and modern state;
- ancestry, phenotype, language, culture, nationality and racial identity;
- archaeological culture, genetic cluster and named historical community;
- historical continuity with unchanged identity;
- indigenous knowledge with a universal or timeless “African worldview”.

## 2. Research architecture

The programme is organised as a connected sequence:

1. **BLACK HINTERLAND / DEEP TIME:** geology, ecology, hominin evolution and early populations.
2. **ANCESTRAL GEOGRAPHIES:** Palaeolithic and Holocene lifeways, migration, settlement, pastoralism and agriculture.
3. **PEOPLES, LANGUAGES & KNOWLEDGE:** identities, endonyms, language histories, technologies, philosophy and memory.
4. **CIVILISATIONS & NETWORKS:** political formations, cities, trade, intellectual exchange and regional systems.
5. **BEFORE THE NAME AFRICA:** historical terminology, external geographical descriptions and the changing scope of “Africa”.
6. **AFRICA BEFORE BALKANISATION:** precolonial territorial systems, colonial conquest and border-making, resistance and postcolonial states.
7. **AFRICA TODAY:** demographics, political economy, institutions, living conditions, technology, conflict, culture and diaspora.
8. **AFRICAN FUTURES — BACK TO THE FUTURE:** historical recovery, contemporary capacity, sovereignty, foresight and futures to 2100.

These are research layers, not a claim that all regions followed the same sequence.

## 3. Deep-time chronology: working orientation

Dates are approximate and must be tied to the particular site, dating method, stratigraphy and uncertainty. “Years ago” and calibrated years BP must not be silently interchanged.

| Period | Research orientation |
|---|---|
| Millions of years ago | African geological, climatic and ecological histories; hominin evolution and changing habitats |
| c. 3.3 million years ago | Lomekwi 3 stone-tool evidence in West Turkana; toolmakers are not identified as Homo sapiens |
| c. 2 million–300,000 years ago | Diverse hominins, stone technologies, ecological adaptation and population dispersals |
| c. 315,000 years ago | Jebel Irhoud, Morocco: early Homo sapiens lineage evidence |
| c. 233,000 years ago | Omo I, Ethiopia: revised minimum age; not an exact species-origin date |
| Middle Stone Age / Middle Palaeolithic | Regionally varied technologies, material transport, pigments, symbolic practices and social networks |
| Late Pleistocene | Population movements, ecological change and diverse cultural histories |
| Holocene, from c. 11,700 years ago | African Humid Period, changing Sahara, pottery, fishing, foraging, pastoralism, agriculture and settlement |
| Later Holocene | Regional state formation, trade, metallurgy, language change and diverse political institutions |
| First millennium BCE onward | Increasing written and external documentary records for particular regions; written visibility is not the beginning of history |
| Classical, medieval and early modern periods | Changing regional names, polities, scholarship, trade, migrations, conflict and diasporic connections |
| 19th–20th centuries | Imperial expansion, colonial rule, partition, anticolonial struggles and independence |
| 21st century | Contemporary African states, AU institutions, Global Africa and competing development and sovereignty trajectories |

No fossil, tool or site should be described as “the first” without stating “oldest currently known in the relevant sample/record” and the date of the evidence review.

## 4. Regional geography — not modern national boxes

Use ecological and historical regions alongside current state boundaries. A region may span several modern states, and one modern state may contain many distinct historical regions and peoples.

- **North Africa and Maghreb:** Mediterranean coast, Atlas, Nile westward regions, Saharan margins and Amazigh histories.
- **Sahara and Sahel:** palaeolakes, oases, pastoral and foraging landscapes, caravan systems and changing climatic corridors.
- **Northeast Africa and Nile Valley:** Nile cataracts, Nubia/Kush, Egypt, Red Sea corridors and the Horn.
- **West Africa:** Niger, Senegal and Volta systems, forest zones, savannas, Atlantic coast and inland networks.
- **Central Africa:** Congo Basin, forest–savanna mosaics, river systems and varied settlement and mobility histories.
- **East Africa and Horn:** Rift Valley, Turkana Basin, Ethiopian and Somali highlands, Red Sea and Indian Ocean connections.
- **Great Lakes:** lake basins, highlands, interlacustrine histories, agricultural and pastoral systems.
- **Southern Africa:** Kalahari, Limpopo, Zambezi, Cape and southern plateau; hunter-gatherer, herder, farmer and state histories.
- **Island and oceanic Africa:** Madagascar, Comoros, Zanzibar, Pemba, Seychelles, Mauritius, Cabo Verde, São Tomé and Príncipe and other island histories.
- **Global Africa:** distinct African-descended communities and diasporas formed through forced displacement, migration, trade, cultural creation and political movements.

## 5. East Africa and the Turkana Basin: first intensive regional case

The Turkana Basin is a priority case because it preserves a long record of hominin evolution, stone technologies, environmental change and later pastoral and community histories. It is not a proxy for all East Africa or all Africa.

Research localities and pathways include:
- **Lomekwi 3 / West Turkana:** early stone-tool manufacture dated to c. 3.3 million years ago.
- **Koobi Fora / East Turkana:** extensive hominin fossil, archaeological and palaeoenvironmental records.
- **Kanapoi:** early hominin fossils and environmental sequences.
- **Nariokotome:** KNM-WT 15000, commonly called Turkana Boy, an important Homo erectus/ergaster skeleton.
- **Ileret:** fossil footprints and archaeological contexts.
- **Lothagam:** palaeontological and archaeological sequences.
- **Namorotukunan:** reported 2025 Oldowan evidence; record the specific study, dating context and limits before broader claims.
- **Lake Turkana and Omo–Turkana basin:** ecological change, pastoralism, mobility, fisheries, trade, land and contemporary community histories.

Later regional work must distinguish Turkana identity from the broader Ateker relational/linguistic frame. Do not treat Ateker as a synonym for Turkana or presume a single origin narrative. Community accounts, linguistic hypotheses, archaeology and genetic findings answer different questions.

## 6. Early populations, ancestry and the category Black

African human evolutionary history is geographically distributed and methodologically active. Fossils, archaeological assemblages, ancient DNA, present-day genetics, morphology and linguistic evidence have different sampling biases and answer different questions.

Core examples for study:
- Jebel Irhoud, Morocco (~315 ka): early Homo sapiens lineage evidence.
- Omo Kibish, Ethiopia: Omo I minimum age revised to ~233 ka.
- Herto, Ethiopia: early Homo sapiens fossils.
- Lomekwi 3, Kenya (~3.3 Ma): stone tools predating Homo sapiens.
- Olorgesailie, Kenya: Middle Stone Age technologies, pigment and non-local raw-material transport.
- Blombos Cave, South Africa: engraved ochre and other material evidence in Middle Stone Age contexts.
- Takarkori, Libya: two directly dated Holocene individuals analysed in the 2025 ancient-DNA study.
- Southern African ancient genomes: 28 individuals in a 2026 Nature issue study, with sampling and interpretation limits.
- Lipson et al. (2022): ancient DNA and deep population structure in sub-Saharan African foragers.
- Bantu-speaking expansions: linguistic and demographic processes with regional variation, admixture and multiple histories, not a biological race or uniform replacement.

**Genetic safeguards**
1. Modelled ancestry proportions are conditional on samples, reference panels and statistical models; they are not ethnic identities.
2. Two individuals cannot represent an entire region or people.
3. Genetic continuity does not prove cultural, linguistic or political stasis.
4. “No detectable signal” is bounded by method, sample coverage and statistical power.
5. Do not infer intelligence, moral worth, authenticity, language or culture from ancestry.
6. Ancient DNA and human remains require ethical governance, provenance, community consultation and applicable permissions.

## 7. The African Humid Period and environmental histories

The Sahara has undergone major climatic and ecological transformations. During the African Humid Period, parts of today's hyper-arid Sahara contained lakes, wetlands, grasslands and habitats supporting human and animal populations. The timing and end of humid conditions varied geographically.

Investigate:
- palaeolakes, river systems, groundwater and changing coastlines;
- hunting, fishing, gathering, pottery and pastoralism;
- livestock histories and the spread of herding practices;
- aridification, mobility, settlement reorganisation and adaptation;
- climate mechanisms and local ecological feedbacks.

Do not describe the Sahara as either an eternal barrier or a uniform green paradise. Avoid simple “desertification caused migration” narratives without site-specific evidence.

## 8. Before the historical name Africa

The word “Africa” has a disputed etymology and a changing geographical scope. Roman use is historically associated with the region around Carthage and the Roman province established after 146 BCE; its application to the whole continent developed over time. Greek “Libya” and “Aithiopia”, Egyptian geographical terms, and later Arabic geographical expressions referred to different places and populations in different contexts.

Research each term by:
- original spelling/script and language;
- date and source genre;
- speaker/writer and intended audience;
- territory or people denoted at that date;
- whether it is an endonym, exonym, administrative term or later scholarly label;
- changes in scope across time.

Terms to investigate include Kemet, Ta-Seti, Kush, Punt, Tjehenu/Tjehemu, Libu, Afri, Africa, Libya, Aithiopia/Ethiopia, Ifriqiya and Bilad al-Sudan. They are not interchangeable and none should be assumed to have named the entire continent.

**Alkebulan:** record as a modern popular attribution whose claim to be the oldest or universal ancient indigenous name for Africa is not established by a presently identified primary source. It may be studied as a contemporary identity expression without presenting the historical claim as fact.

The name did not create African peoples, and written naming evidence is not the start of their histories.

## 9. Peoples, languages, political formations and knowledge

Research endonyms and exonyms, clans and lineages, language histories, place names, polities, institutions, ecological knowledge, technologies, intellectual traditions and oral memory in their own contexts.

Do not map modern language families one-to-one onto archaeological cultures, genetic clusters or ethnic identities. Classifications such as Nilo-Saharan remain debated; “Khoisan” must not be treated as one established language family. Bantu describes a major language-family grouping and associated historical expansions, not a biological race.

Political forms include kingdoms, empires, city-states, councils, confederations, age-set systems, lineage institutions, pastoral governance, merchant networks, religious and scholarly institutions, village assemblies and decentralised formations. Centralised statehood is not the sole measure of complexity.

Knowledge domains include food systems, metallurgy, ceramics, textiles, architecture, medicine, astronomy, mathematics, navigation, ecology, water management, oral literature, law, music and movement. Evaluate actual evidence, methods, labour, access, ownership, transmission and limitations; do not romanticise or attribute modern science retrospectively.

## 10. Balkanisation, colonial transformation and modern state formation

The research must not treat the Berlin Conference (1884–1885) as the single origin of all African borders or fragmentation. Earlier and later processes included coastal expansion, conquest, treaty-making, imperial competition, slave trades, local state formation, resistance, colonial administration and postcolonial boundary negotiation.

For each region, document:
- precolonial territories and political institutions;
- earlier regional and long-distance networks;
- specific colonial powers, dates, administrative decisions and local intermediaries;
- land alienation, labour systems, censuses, ethnographic classification and legal changes;
- African resistance, negotiation, collaboration and adaptation;
- border-making and its local effects;
- post-independence decisions, institutions and regional integration.

Colonial maps and censuses are evidence of the categories and purposes of their makers, not neutral descriptions of timeless societies. African agency and internal political decisions must also remain visible.

## 11. Africa today — dated statistical and institutional baseline

**Snapshot date:** 22 November 2026. This section is a source and indicator baseline, not a complete live statistical dashboard. Use the latest available release for each indicator and preserve the reference year, publication date, geography, denominator, method and uncertainty.

### Demography
Use the UN Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Division, **World Population Prospects 2024** as the official estimates/projections baseline. Its coverage is 237 countries or areas, with historical estimates and projections to 2100. Do not mix UN, World Bank, AU and national population series without explaining differences.

Track population size and age structure, fertility, mortality, life expectancy, migration, urbanisation, dependency ratios, household composition and gender/age differences. Distinguish the whole continent from Sub-Saharan Africa, North Africa, AU membership and UN statistical regions.

### Economy and public finance
The African Development Bank's **November 2026 Macroeconomic Performance and Outlook** projects average African growth of **4.6% in 2026 and 4.5% in 2027**, compared with 4.5% in 2025. These are forecasts, not final outturns. The same update identifies uneven impacts from geopolitical tensions, technology-led global growth and climate risks. Always prefer the latest dated revision over an earlier forecast.

Track GDP per capita, productivity, inflation, employment, informal work, wages, poverty, inequality, public debt, debt service, tax capacity, trade composition, intra-African trade, foreign exchange, investment and capital flows. GDP growth is not a proxy for household welfare or equitable distribution.

### Connectivity and infrastructure
ITU **Facts and Figures 2026** reports internet use at **42% for Africa** in its regional grouping, against 76% worldwide. The 2026 report's regional definitions must be checked before comparison with AU, UN or World Bank aggregates. Track actual use separately from network coverage, affordability, device access, quality, electricity, rural access, gender and age gaps.

Track electricity access, generation mix, reliability, transmission, clean cooking, roads, rail, ports, water, sanitation, housing and logistics.

### Conflict and displacement
Use UNHCR, IOM, OCHA and national sources with clear dates and category definitions. Keep refugees, asylum-seekers, internally displaced people, returnees and stateless people distinct. Regional totals must not be presented as continent-wide totals. Conflict and displacement figures change rapidly.

### Governance and continental institutions
The African Union has 55 member states. The **Agenda 2063 Second Ten-Year Implementation Plan (2024–2033)** is the current delivery phase, structured around seven Moonshots. Treat these as policy ambitions and evaluate implementation, financing, monitoring and citizen-level outcomes.

### Kenya
Use the Constitution of Kenya (2010), Kenya National Bureau of Statistics, national budget and audit documents, county records and relevant African/regional datasets. Track sovereignty, citizenship, language, culture, land, community land, devolution, public finance, employment, education, health, energy, infrastructure, climate and regional inequality. National averages must not erase county and community-level realities.

### Global Africa
Include African-descended populations and diasporas without assuming uniform identity or experience. Study racialisation, slavery and its afterlives, migration, citizenship, remittances, culture, political movements, reparations and continuing relationships with African societies.

## 12. African futures — Back to the Future

**Back to the Future** is a research and foresight proposition: historical memory is a resource for future-making, not a command to restore an idealised past. Recover, test, preserve, adapt and extend knowledge; distinguish inherited practice from contemporary interpretation and new invention.

Research traditions distinctly: Afrofuturism, Africanfuturism, Indigenous Futurisms, Pan-Africanism, decolonial futures, climate/eco-futurism and solarpunk are related but not synonyms.

### Futures domains
1. **Political sovereignty:** institutions, constitutionalism, accountability, security and diplomacy.
2. **Economic sovereignty:** domestic resource mobilisation, industrialisation, value addition, finance and fair trade.
3. **Energy sovereignty:** generation, grids, storage, microgrids and reliable access.
4. **Computing sovereignty:** operating systems, open hardware, semiconductors, data infrastructure, AI governance and local-first systems.
5. **Industrial capability:** materials, machine tools, electronics, medicines, robotics and manufacturing.
6. **Food and biological sovereignty:** agriculture, seed systems, biodiversity, public health and biotechnology.
7. **Space and communications:** satellites, earth observation, astronomy, radio, fibre and optical systems.
8. **Ecological futures:** water, land, climate adaptation, restoration and just transitions.
9. **Cultural and knowledge sovereignty:** languages, archives, universities, publishing, arts, intellectual property and community custodianship.
10. **Human futures:** health, education, housing, labour, gender justice, youth opportunity and dignity.
11. **Continental and diaspora relations:** AfCFTA, regional communities, mobility, payments, shared research and Global Africa.

### Foresight discipline
For every future claim, label it as:
- **OBSERVED:** current measured or documented condition.
- **ACTIVE:** funded/underway project or policy, with source and status.
- **FORECAST:** quantified projection with model, assumptions and horizon.
- **SCENARIO:** plausible internally coherent future, not a prediction.
- **ASPIRATION:** stated goal or desired outcome.
- **SPECULATIVE:** imaginative proposition requiring no claim of present feasibility.

Use 2030, 2033 (Agenda 2063 STYIP), 2040, 2063 and 2100 horizons. Scenarios should include continuity, reform, fragmentation/shock and transformative alternatives. State who benefits, who bears costs, what capabilities are required and what evidence would invalidate the scenario.

## 14. Evidence, ethics and editorial protocol

For every substantive claim record: claim; date/period; geography; people/language; source; source type; original passage/data where accessible; method; sample; uncertainty; competing interpretation; confidence label; maturity label; access date; and next verification action.

**Claim labels:** VERIFIED · CORROBORATED · DISPUTED · UNVERIFIED · INTERPRETATION · OPINION · TESTIMONY · PROPOSAL.  
**Maturity:** LIVE · PROTOTYPE · RESEARCH · CONCEPT · HISTORICAL · SUPERSEDED · UNKNOWN.

Minimum standards:
- primary evidence where available; scholarship and institutional synthesis for context;
- exact citations and page/table/DOI where possible;
- no unsupported universal claims, invented quotations, invented translations or fabricated indigenous terminology;
- distinguish self-identification, neighbour labels, external descriptions and colonial classifications;
- no one source or one individual stands for an entire people;
- respect community authority, informed consent, privacy, restricted knowledge and sensitive site locations;
- no republishing images, maps, scores, manuscripts or datasets without rights review;
- no claim of fieldwork, interview, permission, dataset acquisition or implementation unless documented;
- preserve disagreement and correction history;
- AI summaries are research aids, not sources or independent verification.

## 17. Working thesis

The histories of African peoples predate the geographical name Africa, modern racial classification, colonial cartography and nation-states. They are plural, interconnected and continually changing. The contemporary task is to understand how those histories relate to present institutions and lived realities, and how African peoples can shape futures through knowledge, cultural continuity, political agency, industrial capability and ecological responsibility.
