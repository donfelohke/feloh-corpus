# #NotYetUhuru — The Unfinished Independence
## Research dossier: Kenya · Continental Africa · Global diaspora

**Version:** 1.0  
**Research cut-off:** 24 November 2026  
**Status:** RESEARCH — initial evidence baseline; not a completed audit  
**Editorial line:** Formal independence. Unfinished sovereignty.  
**Prepared by:** Felix Lwova · Don Feloh · @donfelohke

---

## 1. Purpose and central question

This dossier investigates the distance, if any, between formal independence and the effective capacity of people and public institutions to determine political priorities, govern resources, exercise rights and hold power accountable.

**Central question:** How is sovereignty constituted, exercised, constrained and made accountable in Kenya, across African institutions and within transnational diaspora relationships?

The programme treats sovereignty as a set of related but distinct questions. It does not assume that international cooperation, borrowing, trade or migration automatically negate sovereignty. It asks what the relevant arrangements provide, what they cost, who decides, who benefits, what obligations attach and what remedies exist.

## 2. Scope and analytical vocabulary

| Dimension | Working definition | Evidence to examine |
|---|---|---|
| State sovereignty | Kenya's and other states' legal and international status | Constitutions, treaties, diplomatic and international records |
| Popular sovereignty | Authority originating with the people | Elections, participation, petitions, accountability and remedies |
| Constitutional sovereignty | Exercise of public power under constitutional limits | Laws, judgments, institutional mandates and implementation |
| Institutional autonomy | Capacity of public bodies to perform their legal mandates | Appointments, budgets, operational records and oversight |
| Economic sovereignty | Capacity to shape economic choices and productive capability | Revenue, debt, trade, ownership, technology and industrial data |
| Civic sovereignty | Practical ability to organise, dissent, speak and participate | Rights law, protest records, policing, media and access to justice |
| Continental agency | Capacity of African institutions and states to set and implement shared priorities | AU decisions, budgets, regional trade and implementation reports |
| Diaspora participation | Rights and practical channels for citizens abroad to participate | Electoral rules, registration, remittances, investment and political-finance records |

These are analytical categories, not a scoring system. No country, government, party or leader will receive an overall sovereignty score or political ranking.

## 3. Research propositions to test

These are hypotheses, not findings:

1. Formal state independence does not by itself establish effective popular control over public institutions.
2. Colonial institutional inheritance may persist, change or be displaced in different policy areas; continuity must be demonstrated institution by institution.
3. Economic autonomy depends on productive capacity, public revenue, financing terms, bargaining power and accountability—not simply on whether capital is domestic or foreign.
4. Constitutional rights require practical institutional access, protection and remedies to be meaningful.
5. Continental integration can expand collective bargaining and market access, but only where agreements are implemented and benefits are examined.
6. Diaspora participation includes more than remittances or campaign donations; voting, labour rights, family networks, investment and knowledge exchange require separate analysis.

Counter-evidence must be actively sought for every proposition.

## 4. Kenya: historical and institutional investigation

### 4.1 Historical frame

Use the following chronology as an organising structure, not as a substitute for a detailed historical account:

- **1895:** British administration of the East Africa Protectorate begins.
- **1920:** Kenya becomes a Crown Colony; protectorate and colony arrangements must be distinguished in legal and administrative records.
- **1920s–1940s:** settler land policy, labour controls, taxation, African political organisation and changing administrative structures.
- **1952–1960:** Emergency rule, Mau Mau resistance, detention, counterinsurgency and associated human-rights abuses.
- **1960–1963:** constitutional negotiations and transfer of power.
- **12 December 1963:** independence.
- **1963 onward:** post-independence adaptation, retention, reform or replacement of inherited institutions.
- **2010:** promulgation of the Constitution of Kenya, establishing a renewed constitutional settlement and devolution framework.
- **2013 onward:** implementation of devolved government and constitutional institutions.
- **2024–2026:** public-finance protests, civic mobilisation, rights and accountability debates, and preparation for the 2027 electoral cycle.

Research questions:

- Which land, policing, administrative, labour and security arrangements continued after independence?
- Which were abolished, transformed or constitutionally constrained?
- How did land settlement, redistribution and historical land claims evolve?
- How did executive power, party competition and devolution change over time?
- How do oral histories and community records differ from official colonial and post-colonial accounts?

Use colonial archives critically: administrative records reveal what officials recorded and how they classified people; they are not neutral or exhaustive accounts of African experience. Triangulate them with Kenyan archives, oral testimony, scholarship, legal records and community histories.

### 4.2 Constitutional framework

The Constitution of Kenya, 2010, is the primary legal reference. Start with:

- Article 1 — sovereignty of the people.
- Article 2 — supremacy of the Constitution.
- Article 10 — national values and principles of governance.
- Articles 19–25 — rights and fundamental freedoms.
- Article 27 — equality and freedom from discrimination.
- Article 33 — freedom of expression.
- Article 35 — access to information.
- Article 37 — assembly, demonstration, picketing and petition.
- Article 38 — political rights.
- Article 43 — economic and social rights.
- Article 174 — objects of devolution.
- Article 201 — principles of public finance.
- Article 229 — audit of public funds.

Do not infer implementation from constitutional text alone. Pair each provision with legislation, institutional practice, budgets, decisions, case outcomes and citizen experience.

### 4.3 Public finance and debt

The National Treasury's August 2026 Public Debt Bulletin reports total public debt of KES 13,467.52 billion at the end of August 2026: KES 7,668.39 billion domestic and KES 5,799.13 billion external. The bulletin reports 72.3% of GDP in nominal terms and 66.9% in present-value terms. These are Treasury-reported measures, not independent conclusions about fiscal sustainability.

The Treasury's First Quarterly Economic and Budgetary Review for FY 2026/27 reports public and publicly guaranteed debt of KES 13,532.8 billion at 30 September 2026, comprising KES 7,784.6 billion domestic and KES 5,748.2 billion external. This later reporting period should not be combined with the August bulletin as if the dates and definitions were identical.

Investigate:
- debt stock, composition, maturity and currency exposure;
- interest, principal repayments and debt service;
- creditor and instrument concentration;
- domestic borrowing and private-sector credit;
- revenue mobilisation and tax incidence;
- budget execution, pending bills and procurement;
- loan agreements, guarantees and project outcomes;
- public participation, parliamentary scrutiny and audit follow-up.

Borrowing is not, by itself, proof of lost sovereignty. Examine terms, obligations, alternatives, distributional effects and accountability.

### 4.4 Economic structure

The KNBS 2026 Economic Survey reports real GDP growth of 4.6% in 2025, compared with 4.7% in 2024. It reports nominal GDP of KES 17,577.6 billion in 2025 and manufacturing's contribution at 7.1% of GDP. These figures are starting points for deeper sectoral analysis, not measures of household welfare or economic autonomy by themselves.

Investigate agriculture, manufacturing, energy, logistics, digital infrastructure, finance, labour, informal enterprise, imports, exports, foreign investment and domestic value addition.

### 4.5 Civic space and public accountability

The Kenya Freedom Index, developed by Odipo Dev and Amnesty International Kenya, documents 1,292 protest events between January 2025 and June 2026. It provides a data-led basis for studying peaceful assembly under Article 37.

Research:
- protest causes, locations, participants and outcomes;
- police conduct and use-of-force records;
- deaths, injuries, arrests, disappearances and court proceedings;
- independent investigations and prosecution outcomes;
- media freedom and access to information;
- judicial remedies and accountability.

Distinguish allegations, documented incidents, investigations, charges and judicial findings. Do not infer collective responsibility from the conduct of individuals.

### 4.6 Land and historical justice

Study the Truth, Justice and Reconciliation Commission report, National Land Commission records, court decisions, land registries where lawfully accessible, historical maps, community testimony and current regulations.

Track the pathway from recommendation or claim to investigation, determination, remedy, budget allocation and implementation. A published recommendation is not evidence that a remedy has been delivered.

### 4.7 Electoral institutions and 2027

IEBC's Election Operation Plan 2025–2027 recognises participation by Kenyan citizens residing outside the country and describes collaboration with the National Treasury, Ministry of Foreign Affairs and diaspora associations to support registration and voting.

Research the plan, applicable regulations, budgets, registration targets, polling locations, procurement, technology assurance, results management and independent observation. Do not present a proposed expansion, technology option or political allegation as an implemented arrangement or established irregularity.

## 5. Africa: continental and regional sovereignty

### 5.1 African Union financing

The African Union convened its 27th Extraordinary Session of the Executive Council dedicated to financing the Union in Accra on 13 November 2026. The AU described financial independence, accountability and implementation capacity as central issues. Its 13 November readout records proposals concerning statutory contributions, progressive implementation of the 0.2% levy on eligible imports, the Peace Fund and institutional efficiency.

Treat proposals, adopted decisions, member-state implementation and audited financial outcomes as separate evidence stages.

Research:
- assessed contributions and actual payment;
- levy legal basis, collection and remittance;
- external partner funding by programme;
- budget execution and audit findings;
- Peace Fund capital, drawdown and replenishment;
- institutional rationalisation and measurable results.

### 5.2 Agenda 2063

Use the Second Ten-Year Implementation Plan (2024–2033), the continental biennial performance reports, national development plans and regional implementation records.

Compare commitments, indicators, baselines, reporting coverage, expenditure and outcomes. Note that continental reports may cover only the member states and indicators for which data were submitted.

### 5.3 Regional integration and AfCFTA

Investigate:
- intra-African trade and value chains;
- customs procedures and non-tariff barriers;
- rules of origin and standards;
- transport, energy and digital connectivity;
- movement of people and labour;
- distribution of gains and adjustment costs;
- implementation at national border and customs agencies.

Do not equate treaty signature or protocol ratification with operational implementation.

### 5.4 Resource governance and external relations

Study minerals, land, water, energy, biodiversity, data infrastructure and strategic transport assets. Compare ownership, licensing, beneficial ownership, tax terms, local content, environmental obligations, technology transfer, labour conditions and public disclosure.

Compare external relationships on their documented terms. Do not presume that any foreign partner is inherently benevolent or extractive.

### 5.5 Reparatory justice

Separate the legal and historical questions concerning enslavement, deportation, colonial violence, land dispossession, cultural restitution, compensation, memorialisation and institutional remedies. Identify the claimant, responsible actor, legal basis, evidence, remedy sought and status of negotiations or implementation.

## 6. Diaspora: citizenship, work, capital and political participation

### 6.1 Electoral participation

Use the Constitution, Elections Act, IEBC policy, Election Operation Plan 2025–2027, regulations and country-specific implementation notices.

Measure eligibility, registration access, geographic coverage, cost of participation, polling access, ballot secrecy, counting, auditability and dispute resolution.

### 6.2 Remittances

The Central Bank of Kenya's monthly formal-channel series reports October 2026 inflows of USD 430.465 million, with January–October 2026 monthly observations available in its dataset. The CBK methodology covers formal channels, including commercial banks and authorised remittance service providers. It is not a complete measure of informal transfers, in-kind support or diaspora wealth.

Analyse source regions, transfer costs, household uses, currency exposure, gender, labour conditions and investment pathways. Keep formal-channel data distinct from household-survey estimates.

### 6.3 Investment and enterprise

Study diaspora bonds, regulated investment products, SME investment, property, venture capital, pension portability, consumer protection, fraud and dispute resolution. Do not assume remittances are available investment capital; household support and investment are different uses.

### 6.4 Political finance and lobbying

For all political formations, examine:
- Kenyan campaign-finance law and applicable regulations;
- party accounts and statutory filings;
- donation sources and limits;
- expenditure reporting and audit;
- foreign lobbying or agent disclosures where applicable;
- intermediaries, conflicts of interest and beneficial ownership;
- consent, transparency and donor privacy.

A filing can establish that a disclosure was made. It does not by itself establish the amount raised, final recipient, lawful use or electoral effect of funds.

### 6.5 Diaspora civic and knowledge networks

Include professional associations, student groups, labour organisations, cultural networks, research partnerships, mutual-aid groups and civic organisations—not only political campaign networks.

## 7. Comparative country programme

Initial cases: Kenya, Tanzania, Rwanda, Ghana, South Africa, Nigeria, Senegal and Ethiopia.

These cases are selected to investigate differing constitutional, federal, regional, resource and economic arrangements. They are not a ranking.

For each country, record:
- colonial and pre-colonial historical context;
- constitutional and electoral structure;
- public finance and debt;
- resource ownership and revenue;
- productive and industrial capacity;
- labour and social protection;
- civic rights and accountability;
- regional and international agreements;
- diaspora policy and participation;
- data availability and source limitations.

Use country-specific research teams or scholarship where possible. Do not flatten distinct peoples, histories, languages or political institutions into one continental narrative.

## 8. Comparative method

Apply a common question set, not a numerical sovereignty score:

1. Who has legal authority?
2. Who makes the practical decision?
3. Who controls the relevant resources?
4. Who benefits and who bears costs?
5. What information is publicly available?
6. What oversight and appeal mechanisms exist?
7. What is the implementation record?
8. What counter-evidence or alternative explanation exists?

For quantitative comparison, record year, unit, population, definition, denominator, collection method and revisions. Avoid comparing incompatible fiscal years, price bases, survey populations or debt definitions.

## 9. Evidence protocol

Use the FELOH Corpus Evidence and Editorial Standards.

Each material claim must identify:
- claim ID and precise wording;
- evidence label;
- source ID and exact source location;
- publication date and access date;
- jurisdiction, period and population;
- method and scope;
- counter-evidence;
- limitations and unresolved questions;
- researcher interpretation, separately marked.

Prioritise laws, official statistics, audited accounts, parliamentary records, court judgments, original datasets and institutional reports. Use academic work and independent journalism for context, investigation and triangulation.

## 11. Limitations at version 1.0

- This is a research architecture and initial evidence baseline, not a completed historical, legal or economic audit.
- No full extraction of every TJRC recommendation or NLC determination has been completed here.
- Current debt figures are Treasury-reported and use their stated dates and definitions.
- The Kenya Freedom Index covers the period stated by its publishers; it is not a complete record of every assembly or every rights violation.
- CBK remittance statistics cover formal channels.
- IEBC plans do not establish that every proposed activity has been funded or completed.
- AU announcements and proposals do not establish member-state compliance or realised financial independence.
- Comparative country coverage remains uneven and requires additional primary-source collection.
- Political claims about individuals and movements require dated, attributable evidence and a right-of-reply approach where appropriate.

## 12. Research conclusion — provisional

Independence is a legal and historical status. Sovereignty also concerns the practical distribution of decision-making power, resources, rights and accountability.

The programme will test that proposition through historical records, constitutional law, public finance, civic evidence, continental institutions and diaspora data. It will publish what the evidence supports, identify what remains contested and avoid substituting slogans for findings.
