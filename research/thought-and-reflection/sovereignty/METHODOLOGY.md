# African Sovereignty — Research Methodology

**Record:** TR-001-METHOD  
**Status:** PROPOSAL · 22 November 2026  
**Approach:** Interdisciplinary qualitative research, with quantitative and technical evidence where appropriate  
**Canonical home:** FELOH Corpus

## 1. Research design

Use a critical, comparative, interdisciplinary design combining conceptual analysis, historical inquiry, legal and policy analysis, structured literature review, political-economic analysis, case studies and ethically governed oral history where necessary.

The unit of analysis must be explicit in every work package: state, institution, people, community, policy, sector, infrastructure system or historical period. Do not move between these levels without explaining the change.

## 2. Methods and their use

| Method | Application | Evidence standard |
|---|---|---|
| Conceptual analysis | Distinguish state sovereignty, popular sovereignty, peoples' self-determination, autonomy, capability and interdependence | Definitions tied to primary texts and scholarly debate |
| Historical analysis | Trace colonial rule, liberation, independence and institutional change | Primary archives, contemporary records and specialist historiography |
| Legal analysis | Interpret treaties, constitutions, statutes and judgments | Exact provisions, jurisdiction, legal status and date |
| Policy analysis | Compare institutional objectives, plans and implementation | Separate stated commitments from independently evidenced outcomes |
| Critical literature review | Compare positions, methods and disputes | Full-text review where available; abstracts/catalogues labelled as discovery only |
| Comparative case studies | Examine selected national, continental and local arrangements | Transparent selection, common questions and contextual limits |
| Political-economic analysis | Study ownership, production, trade, finance, labour and resource governance | Dated datasets, clear units, methods and source provenance |
| Technical systems analysis | Study infrastructure, software, standards, supply chains and maintenance | Versioned technical sources, experiments and operational evidence |
| Oral history / interviews | Document lived experience and community knowledge | Consent, context, speaker authority, transcript verification and limits |
| Reflective inquiry | Record the author's questions, experience and changing positions | Attributed first-person account; not generalised as population evidence |

## 3. Case-study design

Candidate cases:
- Kenya: constitutional popular sovereignty, devolution, community land, public participation and selected infrastructure.
- Ghana: independence, Nkrumah's neo-colonial critique and later political-economic change.
- Tanzania: Ujamaa and self-reliance, including both policy intentions and documented outcomes.
- African Union: member-state sovereignty, continental integration, popular participation and Agenda 2063 implementation.
- Additional comparisons: selected only after the initial review identifies a specific theoretical or empirical need.

Case selection criteria:
1. Relevance to one or more research questions.
2. Availability of primary and secondary sources.
3. Variation in institutional arrangements.
4. Ability to compare like with like across defined periods.
5. Feasibility and ethical access.
6. Avoidance of cases selected merely to confirm the provisional model.

Use a common case-study template: context and period; sovereign subject; formal legal arrangements; institutions and decision processes; ownership and material capability; knowledge and cultural conditions; external relations; local participation; outcomes; counterevidence; limitations.

## 4. Evidence and claim management

Apply the Corpus claim labels: VERIFIED, CORROBORATED, DISPUTED, UNVERIFIED, INTERPRETATION, OPINION, TESTIMONY and PROPOSAL.

For every substantial claim:
- assign a stable claim ID;
- state the claim narrowly;
- cite the exact source passage or data;
- record author, publisher/custodian, date and access level;
- identify the method used to verify it;
- note supporting and contradictory evidence;
- disclose source dependence, limitations and uncertainty;
- record revisions and supersession.

A treaty establishes the text of an obligation; it does not establish implementation. An official policy establishes an institutional commitment; it does not establish an outcome. A testimony establishes what a person reports; it does not automatically establish all external facts in the account.

## 5. Data and comparative discipline

- Use official statistics and specialist datasets where relevant, but inspect definitions, geographic coverage, reference periods and revisions.
- Do not compare unlike indicators or treat projections as observed outcomes.
- Record units, denominators, missing data and uncertainty.
- Distinguish correlation, causation and interpretation.
- Use quantitative measures to test bounded claims, not to create a spurious single sovereignty score.
- Where data is unavailable, record UNKNOWN and specify what evidence would resolve it.

## 6. Oral history, indigenous knowledge and ethics

Before collecting or publishing testimony:
- obtain informed, voluntary consent;
- explain purpose, audience, storage, quotation and translation;
- agree attribution, anonymity and access restrictions;
- respect community protocols, custodianship and collective rights;
- protect personal and sensitive information;
- preserve recordings and transcripts securely;
- allow correction and, where agreed, withdrawal;
- avoid extracting restricted, sacred or culturally sensitive material;
- do not treat one speaker as representative of an entire community.

Where research requires formal ethics review, institutional permission or community authorisation, obtain it before fieldwork. This document does not claim that such approval has been obtained.

## 7. Researcher reflexivity and positionality

The author's reflections may form part of the research record, but must be distinguished from independent evidence. Record when a question arises from personal experience, prior commitment or observation. Actively seek counterevidence and alternative explanations. Do not infer the psychology or motives of people or institutions without suitable evidence.

## 8. Limitations

Expected limitations include uneven archival access, language and translation constraints, incomplete datasets, changing policy, political sensitivity, contested categories, source bias, selection effects and unequal access to community knowledge. Each publication must disclose the limitations relevant to its claims.

## 9. Reproducibility and research records

Preserve source copies or stable references where rights permit; maintain annotated bibliographies, claim registers, case notes, interview protocols and revision history. For datasets, record version, source, date, transformation and analysis method. For technical systems, reuse the specialist repository's exact test and version records rather than duplicating them.

## 10. Publication gates

- G1 — Question: scope, terms and unit of analysis clear.
- G2 — Literature: primary sources and relevant counterarguments reviewed.
- G3 — Evidence: claims traceable and methods documented.
- G4 — Argument: reasoning, limits and original contribution explicit.
- G5 — Publication: human editorial approval, provenance, date and canonical record complete.

A short reflection or opinion piece may be published before all gates, provided its category and status are accurate. It must not be represented as completed research.
