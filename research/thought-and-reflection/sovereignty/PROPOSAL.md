# Flagship Research Proposal — African Sovereignty

**Record:** TR-001  
**Status:** PROPOSAL · 22 November 2026  
**Programme:** 01 — African consciousness, identity and sovereignty  
**Author:** Felix Lwova / Don Feloh  
**Canonical repository:** FELOH Corpus  
**Working title:** *African Sovereignty: Consciousness, Self-Determination and the Material Conditions of Freedom*  
**Subtitle:** *From Political Independence to Collective Agency*

## 1. Abstract

This investigation asks what it means for African peoples to exercise meaningful sovereignty across political, economic, intellectual, cultural, technological and local life. It begins from a distinction between the legal sovereignty of states and the practical capacity of peoples and communities to shape the conditions governing their lives.

The study will critically examine African and international legal frameworks, anti-colonial political thought, African philosophy, political economy, institutional arrangements and selected case studies. It will test a provisional multidimensional framework against primary texts, historical evidence, contemporary policy and counterarguments. It will not assume that external interdependence is inherently domination, that national control is automatically democratic, or that African societies share one political or cultural experience.

**Status note:** This document is a research proposal. It does not report completed fieldwork, a completed literature review, or validated findings.

## 2. Problem statement

The attainment of internationally recognised state independence does not, by itself, establish that a population exercises effective control over political decisions, economic resources, knowledge systems, cultural representation or essential infrastructure.

The investigation will examine how different meanings of sovereignty relate and sometimes conflict:
- state sovereignty and territorial integrity;
- popular sovereignty and democratic legitimacy;
- peoples' self-determination and collective rights;
- community autonomy and local participation;
- economic control and productive capacity;
- intellectual and cultural agency;
- technological capability and infrastructural dependence;
- continental cooperation and pooled authority.

The African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights provides a central normative source: Articles 19–22 address equality of peoples, self-determination, natural resources and development. The African Union Constitutive Act distinguishes defending member-state sovereignty from objectives such as popular participation, integration and common African positions. These legal and institutional distinctions are starting points for analysis, not evidence that the stated rights and objectives are fully realised.

## 3. Central research question

**What political, economic, intellectual, cultural, technological and local conditions enable African peoples to exercise meaningful self-determination?**

## 4. Prioritised research questions

| Priority | Question | Evidence needed |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | What meanings of sovereignty are in use, and how do they differ? | Legal texts, political theory, historical usage |
| 2 | Who is the sovereign subject in each context? | Constitutional law, peoples' rights, community institutions |
| 3 | What changed between colonial rule, independence and later state formation? | Historical records, institutional histories, political economy |
| 4 | What makes political independence materially meaningful? | Ownership, production, trade, finance, fiscal and resource evidence |
| 5 | Who produces, controls and benefits from knowledge? | Research systems, education, language policy, archives and funding |
| 6 | Who owns, operates and maintains essential infrastructure and technology? | Technical systems, supply chains, procurement, standards and skills |
| 7 | How is sovereignty made accountable to people? | Constitutional mechanisms, participation, rights and institutional performance |
| 8 | Under what conditions does continental integration strengthen collective agency? | AU / REC treaties, policy implementation and case evidence |
| 9 | How should local and indigenous communities participate in decisions affecting land, culture and resources? | Constitutional and statutory law, community records, consent and testimony |
| 10 | What evaluative framework can distinguish formal status, capability, agency and outcomes? | Synthesis and comparative testing |

## 5. Provisional analytical framework

The investigation will test whether meaningful self-determination is better understood as a relationship among several dimensions rather than a single legal status.

| Dimension | Questions to investigate |
|---|---|
| Political | Who makes binding decisions? How is authority legitimised and constrained? |
| Economic | Who owns productive assets, controls finance and shapes economic policy? |
| Intellectual | Who sets research agendas, controls archives and produces authoritative knowledge? |
| Cultural | Who defines, preserves and represents identities and cultural practices? |
| Technological | Who controls design, infrastructure, standards, data and maintenance? |
| Material / ecological | Who governs land, energy, food, water and natural resources, and with what ecological limits? |
| Democratic | Can people participate, contest decisions, access remedies and hold institutions accountable? |
| Continental | What authority is pooled, by whom, under what mandate and with what accountability? |

This framework is **PROPOSAL**. The dimensions may overlap, require subdivision or prove inadequate. They must not be presented as a validated theory or used as an unsupported numerical sovereignty index.

## 6. Critical tensions and rival explanations

The study must examine, not assume away, the following tensions:

1. **State sovereignty versus popular sovereignty:** a state may possess external legal independence while citizens or communities have limited influence over decisions.
2. **National autonomy versus continental integration:** pooled authority may increase bargaining power but can also weaken local accountability if governance is remote.
3. **Self-reliance versus interdependence:** modern societies rely on international exchange; the relevant issue is reciprocity, resilience, bargaining power and the distribution of risk and benefit.
4. **Cultural recovery versus essentialism:** heritage and self-description matter, but neither culture nor identity should be treated as static or uniform.
5. **Resource control versus ecological responsibility:** resource governance must consider environmental harm, local rights and future generations.
6. **Technological autonomy versus isolation:** access to external knowledge and collaboration may strengthen capability; dependence is not established merely by importing technology.
7. **Development versus community rights:** state-led projects require scrutiny of land, participation, livelihood, consent and remedies.
8. **Formal rights versus lived outcomes:** legal recognition is important but must be compared with implementation and accessible remedies.

## 7. Initial case-study strategy

The following are candidate cases, not predetermined findings.

- **Kenya:** constitutional popular sovereignty, devolution, community land, public participation, resource governance and technology policy. The Constitution of Kenya (2010), especially Chapter One, supplies a primary legal starting point. The research must then examine statutes, judicial interpretation, institutional practice and lived outcomes.
- **Ghana:** independence, Nkrumah's neo-colonial critique, political and economic change, and the relation between external economic ties and domestic policy capacity.
- **Tanzania:** Ujamaa, self-reliance and development policy, assessed through historical evidence and competing evaluations rather than romanticisation or dismissal.
- **African Union:** member-state sovereignty, peoples' participation, regional integration, common positions and implementation of Agenda 2063.
- **Comparative cases outside Africa:** selected only where a clearly defined comparison helps test a concept; do not imply that cases are interchangeable.

Case selection will be refined after the literature and data audit. Selection criteria: research relevance, source availability, historical comparability, diversity of institutional arrangements, and feasibility.

## 8. Scope and exclusions

Included:
- African states, peoples, communities and continental institutions, with specific historical and geographic boundaries.
- Legal, historical, philosophical, economic, cultural and technological dimensions.
- Comparative cases where their relevance is explicit.
- Both achievements and failures, including internal domination and unequal power.

Excluded or prohibited:
- Treating Africa as one homogeneous nation, civilisation, language or political subject.
- Equating state authority with popular consent.
- Treating every foreign relationship as neo-colonial or every domestic policy as sovereign.
- Treating self-sufficiency as a necessary or sufficient definition of sovereignty.
- Inferring common ancestry, direct intellectual transmission or cultural uniformity from superficial similarities.
- Treating policy aspirations as proof of implementation.
- Using unsupported numerical rankings or scores.
- Publishing restricted community knowledge without appropriate consent and review.

## 9. Proposed contribution

The intended contribution is a carefully bounded conceptual framework that:
1. distinguishes state, popular, peoples', community and continental sovereignty;
2. identifies material and institutional conditions that affect the exercise of self-determination;
3. makes interdependence and power asymmetry analytically distinct;
4. includes knowledge, culture, technology and ecological conditions without reducing them to economic indicators;
5. provides questions and evidence requirements for comparative case analysis.

The study may conclude that some dimensions should be separated, reframed or rejected. That possibility is part of the method.

## 11. Research ethics and limitations

Oral histories and interviews require informed consent, clear publication purpose, respect for community protocols, privacy protections, attribution or agreed anonymity, and a withdrawal/correction procedure. Do not assume that publicly accessible cultural material is unrestricted for reproduction.

Limitations will include uneven archives, language barriers, inconsistent datasets, contested terminology, institutional self-reporting, changing policy, and the difficulty of comparing different historical contexts. Each case must disclose what the available evidence cannot establish.
