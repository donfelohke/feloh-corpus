## 1. Review objective

Map the major legal, philosophical, historical, political-economic, cultural and technological arguments relevant to African sovereignty. Identify what each source can establish, what it cannot establish, which claims depend on other sources, and where the evidence remains incomplete.

This is a structured review plan and starter bibliography, not a claim that every source has been read in full.

## 2. Review clusters

### A. International law and peoples' self-determination

Primary sources:
- United Nations Charter, Article 1(2).
- International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR), Article 1.
- International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR), Article 1.
- Declaration on the Right to Development (1986).
- United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP, 2007).

Questions:
- How is the right-holder defined as “peoples” in each instrument?
- How do political status, economic/social/cultural development and natural resources relate?
- What are the obligations of states and the limits of these instruments?
- How do indigenous peoples' rights to self-determination and local autonomy interact with state sovereignty and territorial integrity?

### B. African regional law and continental institutions

Primary sources:
- African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights, especially Articles 19–22.
- African Union Constitutive Act, especially Articles 3–4.
- Agenda 2063 and the Second Ten-Year Implementation Plan (2024–2033).

Questions:
- How do peoples' rights under the African Charter relate to member-state sovereignty?
- How do the AU's objectives of unity, integration, popular participation and research interact with its principle of sovereign equality and interdependence?
- Which Agenda 2063 commitments are measurable, and what independent evidence exists about implementation?
- What roles do Regional Economic Communities, national institutions and civil society play?

### C. African anti-colonial and political thought

Initial authors and works:
- Kwame Nkrumah, *Neo-Colonialism: The Last Stage of Imperialism* (1965).
- Amílcar Cabral, “National Liberation and Culture” (lecture delivered 1970).
- Frantz Fanon, *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961).
- Walter Rodney, *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (1972).
- Julius Nyerere, *Freedom and Unity* (1966) and related writings on Ujamaa and self-reliance.

Review questions:
- How do these authors define domination, liberation, culture, development and agency?
- Which arguments were responses to specific historical conditions?
- Which predictions or explanations can be tested against later evidence?
- Where do their positions differ or conflict?
- How have later African scholars revised, criticised or extended these arguments?

Bibliographic metadata and editions for the latter three titles remain to be independently accessioned before formal source records are created.

### D. African philosophy and epistemic authority

Initial sources:
- H. Odera Oruka (ed.), *Sage Philosophy: Indigenous Thinkers and Modern Debate on African Philosophy* (1990).
- Kwasi Wiredu, “Toward Decolonizing African Philosophy and Religion” (1998).
- Paulin Hountondji, *African Philosophy: Myth and Reality* (English edition metadata and edition to be checked).

Review questions:
- What counts as philosophical reasoning, and how can it be documented without romanticising or dismissing oral traditions?
- How do conceptual categories, language and translation affect philosophical inquiry?
- How do African philosophers debate ethnophilosophy, professional philosophy, sagacity and intellectual autonomy?
- Who controls the institutions and archives through which philosophy becomes visible?

### E. Political economy and institutional analysis

Research areas:
- Dependency and neo-colonialism.
- State capacity, public institutions and democratic accountability.
- Trade, debt, finance, investment and resource governance.
- Industrial policy, production, labour and value chains.
- Cooperatives, commons and public goods.
- Distribution of costs, benefits and environmental risks.

The review must include competing political-economic explanations rather than treating dependency theory as a complete account. Select peer-reviewed African political economy scholarship and official data after the initial conceptual review; document the country, time period, measure and limitations of each dataset.

### F. Indigenous and community self-determination

Primary sources:
- UNDRIP, especially Articles 3–5, 11–13, 18–20 and 46.
- Constitution of Kenya (2010), including popular sovereignty, devolution, land and community land provisions.
- African Charter provisions and relevant national legislation/case law.

Research questions:
- How are local decision-making institutions recognised and constrained?
- What does free, prior and informed consent mean in specific legal and community contexts?
- How are language, cultural continuity, land and natural resources governed?
- How do local rights interact with national development policy?

### G. Technology, infrastructure and knowledge systems

Review areas:
- Infrastructure ownership and governance.
- Software freedom, standards, interoperability and reproducibility.
- Data governance, surveillance, platform concentration and digital public infrastructure.
- Hardware manufacturing, supply chains, repair and skills.
- Technical dependency versus beneficial international collaboration.

This cluster should reuse WARENA's specialist source work and must not create a second technical bibliography where one already exists. The sovereignty review should cite the canonical specialist records and only add sources needed for its cross-domain argument.

### H. Critical and comparative literature

Include African feminist political thought, environmental justice, political ecology, democratic theory, international political economy, science and technology studies, and comparative sovereignty research.

Potential authors for structured review include Claude Ake, Samir Amin, Amina Mama, Ifi Amadiume, Mahmood Mamdani, Achille Mbembe and Sylvia Wynter. These names are a discovery queue, not an assertion that they share one theoretical position. Create source records only after verifying exact works, editions and relevance.

## 3. Source review protocol

For every source, record:
1. Full title, author/editor/institution, publication date and edition.
2. Source type, language, publisher or custodian, stable URL/identifier.
3. Date accessed and whether full text, abstract, catalogue record or search extract was inspected.
4. Research question and key claims.
5. Exact passage, article, page, section or dataset supporting each claim.
6. Method and evidence used by the source's author.
7. Supporting and conflicting sources, including source dependence.
8. Scope, omissions, bias, limitations and rights restrictions.
9. Corpus evidence label and confidence/verification status.
10. Relation to a claim ID and the flagship research questions.

## 4. Initial primary-source map

| Source | Key provisions / use | Review caution |
|---|---|---|
| UN Charter | Article 1(2): equal rights and self-determination of peoples | Interpret within the full Charter and international law |
| ICCPR and ICESCR | Common Article 1: self-determination and natural wealth/resources | Analyse treaty obligations and state-party context |
| African Charter | Articles 19–22: equality, self-determination, resources, development | Read exact text and relevant African Commission jurisprudence |
| AU Constitutive Act | Articles 3–4: sovereignty of member states, integration, participation, self-reliance | Distinguish treaty objectives from implementation |
| Right to Development Declaration | Articles 1–3: participation, self-determination, natural resources, state responsibility | Declaration's legal status differs from treaty obligations |
| UNDRIP | Self-determination, autonomy, cultural and institutional rights | Study its status and interaction with other international law |
| Kenya Constitution | Popular sovereignty, devolution, land and community rights | Pair constitutional text with statutes, cases and practice |
| Agenda 2063 | Continental aspirations and current implementation plan | Aspirations and targets are not outcomes |

## 5. Review sequence

1. Read and annotate the primary legal texts.
2. Compare their definitions, rights-holders, obligations and institutional subjects.
3. Read African anti-colonial and philosophical works in full where available.
4. Map disagreements and subsequent scholarly critiques.
5. Select country and institutional case literature based on evidence availability.
6. Review political-economic and technical datasets only after defining the claims they can test.
7. Write a synthesis that distinguishes consensus, disagreement, evidence gaps and the author's own interpretation.

## 6. Review completion criteria

The literature review is ready for synthesis only when:
- the primary legal texts have been read in full;
- major African intellectual sources have been inspected beyond search snippets;
- source metadata and editions are checked;
- each major position has credible counter-literature;
- case selection is justified;
- key claims have traceable passages or data;
- unresolved disagreements and gaps are explicitly recorded.
