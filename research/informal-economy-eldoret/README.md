# Informal Economy — Eldoret and Uasin Gishu

**Status:** RESEARCH · initial evidence and fieldwork design  
**Last reviewed:** 25 October 2026  
**Scope:** Eldoret urban area and wider Uasin Gishu County, Kenya  
**Language:** English is the working language; Kiswahili field materials require human review before use.  
**Owner:** FELOH Corpus research programme  
**Maturity:** Research scaffold; no local field findings have yet been collected or verified.

## Purpose

Build a source-first, locally grounded account of informal economic activity, livelihoods, enterprise conditions and technology needs in Eldoret and Uasin Gishu. The work should describe economic reality without treating informality as synonymous with illegality, lack of skill, or lack of economic value.

The programme is designed to support public-interest research and practical, testable improvements for small enterprises. It must not turn participants into marketing leads or imply that any product or intervention has been validated before testing.

## Research questions

1. Which kinds of work, enterprises and worker–enterprise relationships are present in the selected study areas?
2. How do operators obtain customers, source inputs, price work, manage cash flow and absorb risk?
3. Which costs and constraints matter most: rent, permits, transport, stock, energy, data, platform fees, credit, time, safety or unstable demand?
4. How do gender, age, disability, migration, household responsibilities and access to assets shape opportunities? Collect sensitive information only when justified, voluntary and safely handled.
5. What financial arrangements are used, including mobile money, savings groups, supplier credit, SACCOs and digital credit?
6. How do licensing, market infrastructure, public services and enforcement affect livelihoods?
7. Which digital tools are already used, and what are their total costs, failure modes, privacy risks and offline requirements?
8. What forms of collective purchasing, cooperative services, shared infrastructure or worker representation are desired by participants?
9. Which conclusions are supported by official statistics, which require local measurement, and which remain hypotheses?

## Working definitions

Use the ILO Recommendation No. 204 definition: economic activities by workers and economic units that are, in law or practice, not covered or insufficiently covered by formal arrangements. It includes diverse workers and units across sectors and does not mean all informal activity is illegal.

Keep these units distinct in all records:

- **Informal sector:** a statistical category for qualifying production units.
- **Informal employment:** an employment relationship or job lacking specified formal protections or arrangements, including some jobs inside formal enterprises.
- **Informal economy:** the broader set of relevant economic activities and work relationships.
- **Microenterprise:** a business-size category whose threshold must be stated for the dataset or policy being cited; do not use it as a synonym for informality.
- **Jua kali:** a locally meaningful term whose use and interpretation should be recorded rather than assumed to cover every informal worker.

## Evidence baseline

### National statistical context

The KNBS *Economic Survey 2026* reports estimated informal-sector employment of **18.106 million people in 2025** out of total recorded employment of **21.601 million**. It reports 822,100 additional recorded jobs in 2025, with the informal sector accounting for 87.2% of that increase.

**Interpretation limits:** these are national estimates. The reported total excludes rural small-scale farming and pastoral activities; the 2025 figures are provisional, and the informal-sector total is estimated. These numbers must not be presented as an Eldoret count or as a direct count of all economic activity.

The KNBS 2026 Integrated Labour Force Survey page describes a nationwide survey that includes informal employment and digital-platform employment. Treat the survey as a source to monitor; do not imply its final results are already available unless the published results are located and checked.

### Local policy context

The Uasin Gishu County Integrated Development Plan (CIDP) 2023–2027 and the Uasin Gishu County Urban Institutional Development Strategy 2022–2027 are starting points for local economic development, trade, markets, urban infrastructure and county planning. Plans record intended priorities and targets; they do not, by themselves, prove implementation, completion or impact.

The county Trade, Industrialization, Cooperatives and Enterprise Development page describes policy commitments concerning trader-friendly environments, flexible licensing for some groups, strategic trader stalls, fresh-produce markets, SACCO participation and a county economic forum. These are statements of the county's stated priorities, not independent evidence of delivery.

## Priority populations and work types

Use a broad, non-exhaustive recruitment frame; do not assume any group is uniformly informal.

- Market and street traders; shopkeepers; produce sellers.
- Food vendors, cooks and small hospitality operators.
- Artisans, repair technicians, fabricators and construction trades.
- Hair, locs, nail and other beauty professionals.
- Transport and delivery workers.
- Domestic and care workers, where safe and ethically appropriate.
- Creative workers, performers, makers and independent media workers.
- Digital freelancers, platform workers and small technology providers.
- Small-scale producers and workers linked to agricultural value chains.
- Workers with informal jobs inside otherwise formal businesses.

## Research approach

1. **Desk review:** extract definitions, dates, geographic scope, methods, tables and limitations from official and scholarly sources.
2. **Local systems map:** list candidate markets, streets, workshops, service clusters, transport nodes and business associations. Verify site access, current names and permissions before field visits.
3. **Pilot interviews:** test the interview guide with a small, diverse set of willing participants. A pilot is for instrument refinement, not population-level inference.
4. **Structured fieldwork:** only after ethics, safety, consent, sampling, storage and language arrangements are agreed.
5. **Analysis:** code qualitative evidence; report denominators and missingness for numeric data; separate observed patterns from causal claims.
6. **Validation:** invite participants or relevant local organisations to correct factual misunderstandings without granting any single party control over independent findings.
7. **Publication:** publish aggregated, privacy-protective findings, methods, limitations, source register and a changelog.

## Core indicators

Record only what is needed for the stated question and define every indicator before collection.

| Domain | Example measure | Required qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | Sales in a defined reference period | Gross sales, not profit; state currency and period |
| Costs | Input, rent, transport, energy, fees and other costs | Separate business and household spending where feasible |
| Earnings | Estimated owner earnings after specified costs | State treatment of unpaid labour, depreciation, debt and owner hours |
| Work | Hours, paid workers, unpaid family work, task type | Avoid conflating jobs, people and enterprises |
| Resilience | Ability to meet essential costs during a low-sales period | Use a clearly defined scenario and period |
| Finance | Sources of working capital, repayment burden and fees | Voluntary; do not collect account numbers or credentials |
| Market access | Customer acquisition, repeat business and supplier dependence | Define cohort and period for rates |
| Digital access | Device, connectivity, payment and software costs | Include downtime, fees, data exposure and repairability |
| Safety and rights | Reported hazards, disputes and access to support | Voluntary, confidential and non-identifying in publication |
| Agency | Control over prices, customer relationships, records and tools | Use participant-defined questions; avoid unsupported composite scores |

## Ethics, privacy and data handling

- Participation must be voluntary, informed and unrelated to access to services, business opportunities or public benefits.
- Explain purpose, intended use, recording method, retention period, publication plan and withdrawal limits before consent.
- Do not collect names, phone numbers, precise home locations, M-Pesa account details, PINs, IDs, passwords, private customer lists or identifiable transaction screenshots in public research files.
- Do not publish a quote, photograph or business detail that makes a participant identifiable without explicit, documented permission for that use.
- Keep contact/consent records separate from research data, with access restricted. Do not commit private raw records to this public repository.
- Use aggregation and suppression for small groups or combinations of traits that could identify someone.
- Provide a safe refusal/stop option; avoid interviews during dangerous, highly pressured or coercive situations.
- Do not promise anonymity if the design cannot deliver it. State limits clearly.
- Determine whether institutional ethical review, county permissions or other approvals are required before collecting data. This document is not itself ethics approval.

## What this project does not yet claim

- No representative estimate of Eldoret's informal economy.
- No verified current count of traders, informal workers or microenterprises in Uasin Gishu.
- No completed participant interviews or observations.
- No proof that a county policy or project has been implemented.
- No validated demand for WARENA or any other digital tool.
- No causal claim about technology, formalisation, income or productivity.
- No claim that registration alone improves welfare or that informality is inherently harmful.
