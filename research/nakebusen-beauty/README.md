# NAKEBUSEN Beauty Research Dossier

**Status:** Research scaffold / working dossier  
**Prepared:** 26 October 2026  
**Primary market:** Eldoret, Kenya  
**Business focus:** Natural loc care, retouching and styling, Sisterlocks retie, and artificial loc installation  
**Purpose:** Support evidence-led brand positioning, client trust, service design, web copy, and sustainable beauty work.

> **Editorial rule:** Distinguish documented findings from interpretation, business strategy, owner-provided facts, and unresolved questions. A source's existence does not prove every claim made about a subject. Do not generalise findings from a small sample or from the United States to all Kenyan clients.

## 1. Executive summary

NAKEBUSEN can position beauty as skilled, personal care grounded in respect for the client’s chosen style. The research opportunity is to connect five practical dimensions without overloading the customer-facing brand:

1. **Craft:** reliable loc maintenance, careful styling, clear service boundaries, and honest aftercare guidance.
2. **Identity and agency:** respect for the client’s own reasons for wearing locs; avoid telling clients what their hair must symbolise.
3. **Trust:** transparent prices, clear booking expectations, hygiene, consent before photography, and realistic claims.
4. **Livelihood:** sustainable pricing, professional skill, client retention, and recognition of beauty work as skilled labour.
5. **Digital independence:** accessible communication and booking practices that protect customer data and do not require a large platform or constant connectivity.

**Recommended positioning:** a loc-focused beauty practice offering careful craft, clear communication, and respect for personal style. Heritage and self-determination can deepen the brand story, but should not replace a clear service offer.

## 2. Evidence status and known limits

### Research-backed starting points

- A peer-reviewed study by Edna G. Ndichu and Shikha Upadhyaya examines natural-hair practices as identity work among urban Black women in Kenya. It is directly relevant to Kenyan context, but it is not a survey of every Kenyan consumer and does not establish that every person’s hair choices have the same meaning. [Read the study](https://doi.org/10.1080/10253866.2018.1456427).
- The ILO’s Kenya digital-labour research includes beauty workers in traditional and platform-based work. Its cited beauty-worker samples were 25 traditional-sector workers and 54 platform workers; the report explicitly cautions that the findings are not representative of the whole beauty workforce. Use it to generate questions about platform access and working conditions, not to estimate the national market. [Read the report](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2024-08/ILOP-Digital-Labour-Kenya-v6_0.pdf).
- The ILO’s 2025 market-systems analysis considers beauty and personal care alongside other sectors in Nairobi and Mombasa, with a focus on inclusion of urban refugees and host communities. Its geography and target groups limit direct transfer to Eldoret. [Read the report page](https://www.ilo.org/publications/pathways-market-inclusion-urban-refugees-and-host-communities).
- Hair-discrimination research documents consequences in some contexts, especially the United States. It is relevant comparative material, not proof of the prevalence or legal treatment of hair discrimination in Kenya. [Open-access review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10457631/).

### Not yet established

The following require local research before they are stated as facts:

- The size or growth rate of the loc-care market in Eldoret.
- How many Eldoret clients prefer loc maintenance, Sisterlocks retie, or artificial loc installation.
- Local willingness to pay, competitor prices, client frequency, and the main reasons customers switch providers.
- The prevalence of natural-hair bias in Eldoret schools or workplaces.
- Which loc-care products are safest or most effective for each individual client.
- Any claim that locs have one origin, one meaning, or one uninterrupted tradition across all African communities.

## 3. Research questions and work packages

### WP1 — Hair, identity and self-expression

**Questions**
- How do clients describe their reasons for choosing and maintaining locs?
- Which concerns matter most: appearance, convenience, identity, cultural or spiritual meaning, time, cost, or hair-care routine?
- Do clients experience pressure to alter their hair at work, school, or in family settings?
- How do meanings differ by age, gender, profession, community, and personal history?

**Method:** voluntary, consent-based interviews; anonymised summaries; open-ended questions; no assumption that the researcher knows what a hairstyle means to its wearer.

**Evidence standard:** distinguish individual testimony from claims about a population. Record date, context, question wording, and consent conditions.

### WP2 — Loc-care craft and client outcomes

Document the service process, not just the final photograph.

- Consultation: desired look, maintenance history, sensitivity, time available, and client expectations.
- Service selection: retouch and styling, Sisterlocks retie, or artificial loc installation, subject to practitioner competence and client suitability.
- Care practice: clean tools, hand hygiene, comfortable positioning, breaks when needed, and careful communication about tension or discomfort.
- Aftercare: practical, non-medical guidance; recommend qualified clinical care for persistent scalp pain, inflammation, sores, or unexplained hair loss.
- Follow-up: client-reported satisfaction, style durability, appointment punctuality, and any discomfort.

**Do not promise** guaranteed hair growth, permanent results, treatment of scalp disease, or that a product is “chemical-free” or inherently safe without evidence. “Natural” is not a synonym for safe or effective.

### WP3 — Eldoret beauty-market validation

Do not substitute national hair-product sales for the local market for loc services. Validate demand directly.

**Competitor and alternative map**
- Loc specialists and Sisterlocks practitioners.
- General salons that offer loc maintenance.
- Independent/mobile practitioners, where relevant.
- Self-maintenance and informal referrals as alternatives to paid services.
- Synthetic/artificial loc installation providers.

**Record for each comparable offer**
- Publicly listed services and prices, date checked, location and travel implications.
- Appointment method, response time, service scope, visible portfolio and consent practices.
- Client review themes, separating verified reviews from promotional claims.
- What is included in the price and any extra charges.
- Evidence gaps: do not infer quality from price or social-media followers alone.

**Customer discovery**
Interview a small initial set of current, former, and prospective clients. Treat the first round as qualitative exploration, not a statistically representative survey. Ask what they need, what frustrates them, what makes them trust a practitioner, how often they seek maintenance, and what they would consider fair value. Avoid leading questions that simply invite praise for NAKEBUSEN.

### WP4 — Beauty work, digital platforms and ownership

Investigate how booking and social media help practitioners reach clients, while also considering fees, platform dependence, safety, privacy, and unpaid work.

Useful measures for a small practice:
- Enquiries by channel.
- Enquiry-to-booking conversion.
- No-show and cancellation rates.
- Repeat bookings and time between visits.
- Service time, consumables, travel and overheads.
- Revenue per working hour, not just headline price.
- Client consent for storing contact details and publishing images.

Keep a simple exportable customer record. Collect only what is necessary, limit access, explain how information is used, and obtain explicit permission before using a client’s image or testimonial in marketing. Check applicable Kenyan data-protection requirements before implementing a booking or customer-data system.

### WP5 — Beauty heritage and cultural representation

Build a source-led record of hair practices across specific places, communities, and periods. Prefer dated photographs, museum records, historical documents, community testimony, and specialist scholarship over viral infographics.

For every historical statement, record:
- Community, place, period, and the exact practice being described.
- Source creator, date, provenance, language, translation, and rights.
- Whether the source is a primary record, later interpretation, oral testimony, or promotional retelling.
- Competing interpretations and what remains unknown.

Avoid presenting all African hair traditions as identical. Do not use sacred or restricted knowledge, community-owned images, or client stories without appropriate permission.

## 4. Case studies and what they can support

### Case study A — Natural hair as identity work in urban Kenya

**Source:** Ndichu & Upadhyaya, “Going natural: Black women’s identity project shifts in hair care practices” (2019 issue; published online 2018), *Consumption Markets & Culture*. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10253866.2018.1456427).

**Relevance:** provides a Kenya-specific scholarly basis for investigating how hair-care practices can carry social and identity meanings.

**Supports:** treating natural hair as more than a purely functional consumer choice and asking clients how they interpret their own practice.

**Does not support:** assuming all Black women, all Kenyans, or all loc wearers share one motivation; estimating current Eldoret demand; or treating natural hair as inherently healthier in every circumstance.

**NAKEBUSEN application:** invite clients to describe their own desired look and maintenance priorities. Make the brand welcoming without imposing a political or cultural interpretation on every appointment.

### Case study B — Beauty work and digital platforms in Kenya

**Source:** ILO / Thunderbird Africa, *Digital Labour in Kenya* (2024 publication). [PDF](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2024-08/ILOP-Digital-Labour-Kenya-v6_0.pdf).

**Relevance:** compares aspects of traditional and platform-mediated beauty work, including use of WhatsApp and other social or specialist platforms.

**Supports:** investigating how practitioners find clients, organise work, and experience digital tools.

**Does not support:** national prevalence estimates, the profitability of any particular platform, or a claim that every beauty worker benefits from platform work.

**NAKEBUSEN application:** measure which channels actually generate bookings. Maintain a direct, exportable client relationship instead of assuming reach or followers automatically become revenue.

### Case study C — Market inclusion in Kenya’s beauty and personal-care sector

**Source:** ILO, *Pathways to Market Inclusion for Urban Refugees and Host Communities* (2 December 2025). [Publication and report](https://www.ilo.org/publications/pathways-market-inclusion-urban-refugees-and-host-communities).

**Relevance:** examines barriers and opportunities in beauty and personal care among other sectors in Nairobi and Mombasa.

**Supports:** considering market access, skills, enterprise development, and decent-work constraints.

**Does not support:** direct estimates for Eldoret or a specific loc-care niche.

**NAKEBUSEN application:** use the report to build a checklist of local questions, then collect Eldoret-specific evidence before choosing prices, partnerships, or expansion plans.

### Case study D — Hair bias and wellbeing (comparative evidence)

**Source:** Nkimbeng et al., “The Person Beneath the Hair: Hair Discrimination, Health, and Well-Being” (2023). [Open-access article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10457631/).

**Relevance:** reviews connections between hair discrimination, social conditions, and wellbeing.

**Supports:** recognising that hair-related stigma can have consequences and merits serious research.

**Does not support:** assuming the same forms, frequency, or legal remedies apply in Kenya.

**NAKEBUSEN application:** use respectful, non-stigmatising language. If the brand discusses discrimination, label international evidence accurately and seek Kenyan evidence before making local prevalence claims.

## 5. Loc-care evidence protocol

This dossier is not a clinical guideline. Before making health or product claims, review reliable dermatology and cosmetic-safety sources, and distinguish clinical evidence from practitioner experience.

| Topic | Responsible approach | Claim to avoid without evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Tension and discomfort | Ask about comfort; stop or adjust if the client reports pain or excessive pulling. | “Tight retwists make locs grow faster.” |
| Scalp symptoms | Do not diagnose; refer persistent or severe symptoms to an appropriate health professional. | “This service cures dandruff, alopecia, or scalp disease.” |
| Product use | Record products used and relevant client sensitivities; follow label instructions. | “All natural oils are safe for every scalp.” |
| Maintenance intervals | Agree a practical interval based on style, condition, preferences, and practitioner judgement. | A universal interval guaranteed to suit everyone. |
| Hygiene | Document cleaning practices and use clean tools and materials. | Unsupported claims such as “100% sterile” without a validated process. |
| Results photography | Ask permission for each intended use; allow refusal without affecting service. | Treating a service booking as consent to publish an image. |

Potential reference starting points:
- [American Academy of Dermatology — hair and scalp information](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/hair-scalp-care)
- [US FDA — hair products](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-products/hair-products)
- [US FDA — using cosmetics safely](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/resources-consumers-cosmetics/using-cosmetics-safely)

US sources are useful educational references, not substitutes for Kenyan law or personalised medical advice.

## 6. Brand positioning

### Positioning statement

**NAKEBUSEN is a loc-focused beauty practice for clients who value careful maintenance, considered styling, and clear, respectful service.**

### Brand promise

**Your style. Our craft.**

### Supporting message

**Beautiful locs begin with care, skill, and a style that feels like you.**

Use these as proposed brand copy, not as independently proven claims of superior outcomes. Substantiate quality statements through consistent practice, transparent processes, client feedback gathered with consent, and accurate portfolios.

### Messaging hierarchy

1. **Service clarity:** what is offered, who it is for, price, expected time, and how to book.
2. **Trust:** consultation, careful work, hygiene, transparent communication, and realistic expectations.
3. **Personal expression:** clients choose their own look; the practitioner provides skill and guidance.
4. **Cultural depth:** locs and hair can hold varied personal and community meanings; tell specific, sourced stories rather than claiming one universal history.
5. **Technology:** digital convenience should support the service, not distract from it.

### Avoid

- Claims that all loc wearers share the same cultural, political, or spiritual identity.
- Unverified “ancient African” origin claims.
- Colourism or texture-shaming; no implication that one texture is more professional or beautiful.
- Fear-based sales, unrealistic growth promises, and unsupported medical claims.
- Fake testimonials, invented ratings, or unsourced competitor comparisons.

## 8. Market validation plan

### First 30 days

**Week 1 — establish the baseline**
- Confirm the active location, contact details, services, prices, service conditions, and booking process.
- Record current enquiry and booking sources.
- Audit existing website copy and images for accuracy, mobile usability, and consent.

**Week 2 — listen to clients**
- Conduct short, voluntary interviews with a mix of current, former, and prospective clients.
- Ask about maintenance needs, trust, service expectations, travel, booking friction, and price clarity.
- Do not publish personal stories without separate permission.

**Week 3 — map the local offer**
- Collect dated public information on comparable Eldoret providers and substitutes.
- Record service scope and price inclusions, not just headline prices.
- Mark missing or incomparable data explicitly.

**Week 4 — improve and measure**
- Test a clearer service/pricing page and booking call-to-action.
- Track enquiries, confirmed bookings, cancellations, repeat visits, and approximate contribution per service.
- Review what changed and avoid attributing results to a change without sufficient evidence.

### Minimal measurement sheet

| Metric | Definition |
|---|---|
| Enquiries | Unique service enquiries in the period |
| Confirmed bookings | Appointments explicitly agreed |
| Conversion | Confirmed bookings divided by enquiries |
| Attendance | Completed appointments divided by confirmed bookings |
| Repeat rate | Clients returning within a defined period |
| Average service revenue | Service revenue divided by completed appointments |
| Effective hourly revenue | Service revenue less direct service costs, divided by total service time |
| Client satisfaction | Optional, consistently worded feedback with no pressure to respond |

Set the period and denominator before comparing results. Do not publish client data or infer demographic traits unnecessarily.
