## A. Naming and Roman Africa

### A1. William Nassau Weech, Brian Herbert Warmington, and R. J. A. Wilson, “Africa, Roman.” Oxford Research Encyclopedia / Oxford Classical Dictionary (2016)
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.170
- Type: Scholarly reference chapter.
- Supports: A bounded description of the Roman province of Africa established in 146 BCE in territory corresponding largely to north-eastern Tunisia, and later provincial changes.
- Does not establish: A definitive etymology for the word *Africa*, nor that the Roman provincial name alone explains every later use of the continental name.
- Access/review: Search-result extract inspected; full text not reviewed.
- Follow-up: Review the complete entry and its cited scholarship; compare with historical maps and North African scholarship.

### A2. David Stone, “Roman Africa.” Oxford Bibliographies in Classics (2018)
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0327
- Type: Scholarly bibliography.
- Supports: A research map for Roman-controlled North Africa and its historiography.
- Limitations: Broad regional usage differs from the original province’s boundaries; bibliographic overview is not itself a primary source.
- Access/review: Abstract/extract inspected.
- Follow-up: Select archaeological and historical works from the bibliography.

### A3. Carthage National Heritage / Carthage site, “Africa and its name”
- URL: https://carthage.benmacha.tn/en/afrique
- Type: Heritage interpretation page.
- Supports: A concise account of the province-name history and several proposed etymologies.
- Limitations: A public heritage page is an entry point, not sufficient by itself to settle disputed etymology. Its own account states that the origin hypotheses are not proven.
- Access/review: Page extract inspected.
- Follow-up: Trace each etymology to specialist linguistic and classical scholarship before assigning confidence.

## B. Trade, political organisation, and plurality

### B1. Shadreck Chirikure, “Documenting Precolonial Trade in Africa.” Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History (2017)
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780190277734.013.68
- Type: Scholarly review chapter.
- Supports: The need to combine archaeology, historical linguistics, oral accounts, material culture, and documentary sources to reconstruct intra-African and external trade.
- Limitations: Evidence visibility differs by material; perishable goods and undocumented exchanges may be underrepresented. A continent-wide overview cannot substitute for regional case studies.
- Access/review: Abstract inspected.
- Follow-up: Review complete chapter; extract case studies by route, period, commodity, and evidence type.

### B2. Soeren J. Henn and James A. Robinson, “Africa as a Success Story: Political Organization in Precolonial Africa.” Annual Review of Economics 18 (2026), 447–471.
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-economics-051624-070224
- Type: Scholarly review article.
- Supports: A recent comparative argument about political decentralisation and precolonial African organisation.
- Limitations: Its estimate and explanation of political organisation are scholarly claims requiring examination of definitions, data, and method; do not generalise a continent-wide model to every community or period.
- Access/review: Abstract inspected.
- Follow-up: Read the article and cited datasets; compare with archaeological and regional historical studies, including evidence of states, empires, hierarchies, and coercion.

### B3. Robert Smith, “Peace and palaver: international relations in pre-colonial West Africa.” The Journal of African History.
- URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/abs/peace-and-palaver-international-relations-in-precolonial-west-africa1/AF4FD833016E85A0CE08853C7DD11008
- Type: Scholarly journal article.
- Supports: A research lead on diplomacy, customary law, trade, embassies, and inter-polity relations in precolonial West Africa.
- Limitations: Regional focus; not evidence that all African societies used identical diplomatic systems.
- Access/review: Abstract inspected.
- Follow-up: Read the article and trace its primary-source basis.

### B4. “Endogenous Colonial Borders: Precolonial States and Geography in the Partition of Africa.” American Political Science Review (2025).
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055424000054
- Type: Peer-reviewed research article.
- Supports: A challenge to the simplified claim that every African border was drawn arbitrarily; the authors analyse precolonial political frontiers, geography, treaties, and border formation.
- Limitations: Does not imply colonial borders were consensual, benign, or universally aligned with local identities. Findings are based on the authors’ specified data and methods.
- Access/review: Abstract inspected.
- Follow-up: Review data, methods, and regional cases; compare with historical accounts of coercion and negotiation.

## C. Egyptian and Greek intellectual histories

### C1. Mary R. Lefkowitz, “Egyptian philosophy: influence on ancient Greek thought.” Routledge Encyclopedia of Philosophy (1998).
- URL: https://www.rep.routledge.com/articles/thematic/egyptian-philosophy-influence-on-ancient-greek-thought/v-1
- Type: Scholarly reference article.
- Supports: A map of arguments concerning Greek philosophers’ reported visits to Egypt and claims that Greek philosophy derived from an Egyptian “mystery system.”
- Limitations: The question is contested; ancient testimony, later narratives, and modern reconstructions must be separated. This source represents a particular scholarly perspective, not the entire debate.
- Access/review: Article summary inspected; full argument not reviewed.
- Follow-up: Read the complete entry and competing scholarship; examine the primary ancient texts, dates, genre, transmission, and archaeological context.
- Research rule: Do not turn “contact occurred,” “a source reports a visit,” and “a doctrine was transmitted” into interchangeable claims.

## D. Iranian language and cultural continuity

### D1. Ludwig Paul, “Persian Language i. Early New Persian.” Encyclopaedia Iranica (published 2000; updated 2013).
- URL: https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/persian-language-1-early-new-persian/
- Type: Specialist scholarly reference article.
- Supports: Early New Persian developed over centuries following the Arab-Muslim conquest; its written record used Arabic script while linguistic and cultural continuities remained.
- Limitations: The language’s development cannot be reduced to unchanged survival: it involved transformation, contact, political patronage, and regional differences.
- Access/review: Article extract inspected.
- Follow-up: Read complete article; compare Middle Persian evidence, early New Persian texts, and regional literary histories.

### D2. “The Persian Cosmopolis.” Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Asian History.
- URL: https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61799/chapter/546326702
- Type: Scholarly reference chapter.
- Supports: A research path for Persian’s development as a literary and bureaucratic language across several polities after the conquest.
- Limitations: A transregional Persianate history is not identical to the history of the modern Iranian nation-state.
- Access/review: Search-result extract inspected.
- Follow-up: Read full chapter and use its bibliography for comparative case studies.
