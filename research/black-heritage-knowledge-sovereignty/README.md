# Black Heritage, Historical Identity & Knowledge Sovereignty

**Status:** RESEARCH / PROPOSAL — initial programme, 26 October 2026  
**Owner:** FELOH Corpus research programme  
**Editorial standard:** [Evidence and Editorial Standards](../../EVIDENCE-AND-EDITORIAL-STANDARDS.md)  
**Research index:** [Research README](../README.md)

## Purpose

Investigate how historical naming, diverse African identities, political organisation, trade, intellectual exchange, documentary survival, and contemporary computing shape the reconstruction and stewardship of Black heritage.

The programme treats Black heritage as a diverse, connected, internally contested mosaic—not as a single civilisation, biological category, language, state, or uninterrupted political identity. It aims to improve access to evidence while preserving local self-description, source provenance, cultural rights, and disagreement.

## Core research questions

1. **Naming and classification:** What did *Africa* mean in different historical periods? How did Roman geography, later cartography, colonial administration, and modern political movements change its scope and meaning?
2. **Plural identities and cooperation:** How did communities identify themselves in specific periods? Which political arrangements enabled trade, diplomacy, confederation, or cooperation without requiring continental uniformity?
3. **Trade and mobility:** What do archaeology, historical linguistics, oral accounts, material culture, and written records show about inland, trans-Saharan, Mediterranean, Red Sea, and Indian Ocean networks?
4. **Philosophy and intellectual exchange:** What can be established about African philosophical and literary traditions, Egyptian–Greek contact, and other Mediterranean knowledge exchanges? Which claims rest on primary evidence, later testimony, or modern interpretation?
5. **Conquest and cultural continuity:** How did conquest, religious change, writing systems, institutions, and patronage affect language and cultural continuity in selected comparative cases, including Iran?
6. **Documentary gaps:** Which archives, oral histories, manuscripts, material collections, and community records are inaccessible, endangered, under-described, or not yet digitised?
7. **AI and historical reconstruction:** Where can OCR, transcription, translation, entity linking, and cross-source search assist researchers—and where do hallucination, bias, poor metadata, or missing sources create risks?
8. **Knowledge sovereignty:** What community-governed, local-first, federated infrastructure can preserve access, provenance, privacy, portability, and durable stewardship?
9. **Political economy:** Who owns the infrastructure, data, models, distribution channels, and value created by digitising and interpreting heritage?
10. **Institutional design:** How can independent communities cooperate through shared protocols while retaining authority over their own records, languages, cultural protocols, and participation?

## Working thesis — INTERPRETATION, not established fact

A possible source of weakness in historical recovery is not cultural diversity itself, but the lack of adequately resourced, durable, interoperable institutions for collecting, preserving, verifying, and sharing knowledge across diverse communities.

This hypothesis must be tested against evidence about archives, literacy, state formation, colonial disruption, funding, infrastructure, language access, intellectual property, and existing successful institutions. It must not be used to presume that Pan-Africanism or any other political tradition has universally failed.

## Scope and exclusions

- Treat Africa as a continent with many histories, not a timeless singular nation.
- Do not treat race, ancestry, language, culture, nationality, and political identity as interchangeable.
- Do not infer common origin or direct transmission from similarity alone.
- Do not treat all North African peoples as a single homogeneous or uniformly “Caucasianised” population. Investigate particular societies, periods, and processes with appropriate evidence.
- Do not assume trade was always peaceful, inclusive, or decentralised; investigate coercion, slavery, warfare, hierarchy, and exclusion as well as exchange.
- Do not claim that Greeks wholly copied Egyptians, that Egyptian influence was absent, or that a specific intellectual tradition predates another without claim-specific evidence.
- Do not reduce Islam to a single external imposition or treat Iranian cultural history as uniform.
- Do not assert that AI is being “tamed” to suppress Black historical reconstruction without direct evidence for the specific actor, action, and motive.
- Do not publish restricted, sacred, sensitive, or personally identifying community knowledge without appropriate permission and review.

## Evidence protocol

Use the corpus labels: **VERIFIED, CORROBORATED, DISPUTED, UNVERIFIED, INTERPRETATION, OPINION, TESTIMONY, PROPOSAL**.

For each substantive claim, record:
- claim ID and exact wording;
- period and geographic scope;
- source, author/creator, date, source type, and stable URL or identifier;
- relevant passage, page, dataset, artefact, or archival reference;
- primary/secondary status and source-access level;
- support, counterevidence, dependence on other sources, and limitations;
- alternative interpretations and unresolved questions;
- review date, reviewer, and any correction/supersession history.

A search result or AI summary is a discovery lead, not final verification. AI-assisted transcription, translation, or summarisation must be marked and checked against the underlying source wherever access permits.

## Initial milestones

1. **M0 — Scope and source map:** establish research questions, vocabulary, and initial source register.
2. **M1 — Claim ledger:** separate established facts from interpretations, hypotheses, contested claims, and opinion.
3. **M2 — Case studies:** complete bounded studies for Roman Africa/naming, precolonial trade and political organisation, intellectual exchange, and Persian cultural continuity.
4. **M3 — Archive and infrastructure assessment:** map documentary risks and evaluate community-controlled technical options.
5. **M4 — Review and publication:** peer review, community review where relevant, rights review, reproducible citations, and corrections process.

No milestone is complete merely because this programme document exists.

## Acceptance criteria

- Each headline claim is linked to an inspectable source or explicitly marked unresolved.
- At least one credible counterargument or limitation is recorded for contested claims.
- Primary sources are distinguished from modern scholarship and tertiary summaries.
- Period, geography, and relevant population are stated.
- No claim of complete coverage is made without a documented inventory and coverage method.
- Any technical pilot has separately recorded design, implementation, tests, and observed results.
- Publication respects rights, consent, privacy, cultural protocols, and repository licensing.

## Known limitations at programme start

This is a research design, not a completed historical survey. The initial bibliography is selective. It does not establish a comprehensive inventory of African archives, languages, philosophies, or technologies; does not establish a causal account of Pan-Africanism’s outcomes; and does not establish coordinated suppression of Black history by AI companies or political actors. These questions require further source work.
